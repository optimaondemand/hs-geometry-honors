📚 VOCABULARY

Algebraic Rule: A formula that describes how each point of a figure changes during a transformation.

Congruent: Figures that are the same size and shape with corresponding sides and angles equal.

Coordinate Plane: A two-dimensional surface formed by the intersection of a horizontal x-axis and a vertical y-axis.

Dilation: A transformation that enlarges or reduces a figure proportionally from a center point, usually the origin.

Image: The figure that results from a transformation of a pre-image.

Non-Rigid Motion: A transformation that changes the size of a figure, such as a dilation.

Origin: The point (0, 0) where the x-axis and y-axis intersect on the coordinate plane.

Pre-Image: The original figure before any transformation is applied.

Rigid Motion: A transformation that preserves distance and angle measure, such as a translation, reflection, or rotation.

Scale Factor: The number used to multiply the coordinates of a figure to make it larger or smaller in a dilation.

Similarity: When two figures have the same shape but not necessarily the same size; corresponding angles are equal, and side lengths are proportional.

Transformation: A change in the position, size, or shape of a figure.

Translation: A transformation that slides a figure a specific distance in a given direction.

Vector: A quantity with direction and magnitude that describes how far and in what direction to translate a figure.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore how dilations can be described using algebraic rules in the coordinate plane. You'll learn how to match the geometric movement of a figure with a coordinate-based rule, such as resizing it using a scale factor. Through examples, algebraic representation, and graphing practice, you’ll become confident in both describing and applying these transformations.

 

Dilation Rules and Algebraic Representations

Dilation Enlargements

A dilation resizes the entire figure proportionally from a center (typically the origin).

\((x,y)\rightarrow (kx,ky)\)

where \(k>1\)

Dilation Reductions

A dilation resizes the entire figure proportionally from a center (typically the origin).

\((x,y)\rightarrow (kx,ky)\)

where \(0<k<1\)

Example:

\(\textcolor{purple}{(x,y)\rightarrow (2x,2y)}\)

Example:

\(\textcolor{orange}{(x,y)\rightarrow (0.5x,0.5y)}\)



Example 1

The triangle ABC is transformed to create triangle A'B'C' as shown.

Describe the transformation geometrically.

Represent the transformation algebraically.

Solution:

  • To describe the geometric transformation, we need to observe what the triangle did from the preimage ABC to the image A'B'C'.
  • We notice that the vertices of the triangle moved along a line from the origin. More specifically, they moved closer and the whole image shrank a little bit.
  • This tells us the triangle was dilated by a factor less than one. Now, we need to find the factor it was dilated by.
  • To find the scale factor, we divide the x and y values of a point from the image by the x and y values of the corresponding point in the preimage.
    • We can use the point C and C'
    • \(\frac{C'}{C}\)
    • For the x values, we find \(\frac{3}{4}\)
    • For the y values, we find \(\frac{3}{4}\)
    • If we do this with the coordinates of point A and A', we find \(\frac{-6}{-8}=\frac{3}{4}\)
  • Note that you always divide the coordinates of the IMAGE by the coordinates of the PreImage.
  • In doing so, we find that the scale factor is \(\frac{3}{4}\).
  • Now, we can represent this algebraically using the rule for dilations.

The triangle dilated by a factor of \(\frac{3}{4}\) represented as \((x,y)\rightarrow (\frac{3}{4}x,\frac{3}{4}y)\).



Example 2

Quadrilateral ABCD has the following vertices; \(A(-6,3)\), \(B(-7,7)\), \(C(-2,9)\), and \(D(-1,5)\).

The quadrilateral is dilated by a factor of \(\frac{5}{2}\).

Find the coordinates of the image A'B'C'D'.

Solution:

  • Since we are dilating the quadrilateral by a factor of \(\frac{5}{2}\), we can use the rule \((x,y)\rightarrow (\frac{5}{2}x,\frac{5}{2}y)\).
  • We will apply this rule to each of the vertices to identify the coordinates of the image by multiplying each coordinate by the scale factor.
    • Let's find A' - \(A(-6,3)\rightarrow A'(\frac{5}{2}\cdot -6,\frac{5}{2}\cdot 3)=(\frac{-30}{2},\frac{15}{2})=(-15,7.5)\)
    • \(B(-7,7)\rightarrow B'(\frac{5}{2}(-7),\frac{5}{2}(7))=(-17.5,17.5)\)
    • \(C(-2,9)\rightarrow C'(\frac{5}{2}(-2),\frac{5}{2}(9))=(-5,22.5)\)
    • \(D(-1,5)\rightarrow D'(\frac{5}{2}(-1),\frac{5}{2}(5))=(-2.5,12.5)\)

Answer: The coordinates of A'B'C'D' are \(A'(-15,7.5)\), \(B'(-17.5,17.5)\), \(C'(-5,22.5)\), and \(D(-2.5,12.5)\).



Example 3

Quadrilateral ABCE has vertices as shown.

Draw the image of the quadrilateral that is dilated by a factor of 3 about the origin.

Solution:

  • We can begin by applying the rule \((x,y)\rightarrow (3x,3y)\) to each vertex of the quadrilateral to find the new vertices.
    • \(A(-2,1)\rightarrow A'(3\cdot -2,3\cdot 1)=(-6,3)\)
    • \(B(-2,4)\rightarrow B'(3\cdot -2,3\cdot 4)=(-6,12)\)
    • \(C(2,4)\rightarrow C'(3\cdot 2,3\cdot 4)=(6,12)\)
    • \(C(2,1)\rightarrow C'(3\cdot 2,3\cdot 1)=(6,3)\)
  • Now that we know the coordinates of the image, we can plot each point. Then we can connect the points to draw the quadrilateral.



Conclusion

In this lesson, you learned how dilations can be represented using algebraic rules in the coordinate plane. Dilations resize figures from the origin using a scale factor with the rule \((x,y)\rightarrow (kx,ky)\). You practiced identifying these transformations from given coordinates, writing transformation rules, and graphing new images. Mastering these skills helps you describe geometric movements precisely and prepares you for analyzing transformation sequences and congruence or similarity in future lessons.
