Assignment · 20 pts

In this assignment you will design and create your own tessellation and then analyze it using the language of symmetry from this lesson. Your work has two parts: first, you will build the tessellation itself; second, you will answer a set of written questions explaining the mathematical structure behind what you created. Both parts are required for full credit.

What You Will Turn In

  • Your completed tessellation — either as a photograph of a hand-drawn paper design or as a saved file / screenshot from Desmos or GeoGebra.
  • Your completed written responses to all questions in Part 2, submitted in the same document or Canvas text entry.
📋 PART 1 — CREATE YOUR TESSELLATION

What You Need to Create

Design and draw a tessellation — a pattern that covers the plane with no gaps and no overlaps. Your tessellation must meet all of the following requirements:

  • Your tessellation usesat least two different types of symmetryfrom this lesson: reflection symmetry, rotational symmetry, or translation symmetry. You must be able to identify and describe each one you use.
  • Your design showsat least 12 complete tiles(copies of your repeating shape or shapes) so that the repeating structure of the pattern is clearly visible.
  • The tiles fit togetherwithout gaps or overlaps. Edges must line up cleanly.
  • Your tessellation isneat and clearly drawn— lines are distinct, shapes are recognizable, and the pattern is easy to follow.


Shape Requirements

You may use any shape or combination of shapes that tessellates the plane. You are not limited to regular polygons. The following are all acceptable choices — pick whichever interests you, or design something more creative:

  • A single regular polygon (equilateral triangle, square, or regular hexagon — these are the only regular polygons that tessellate on their own)
  • Two or more different regular polygons used together (a semi-regular tessellation)
  • A non-regular polygon such as any triangle, any quadrilateral, or certain hexagons
  • A modified or custom shape created by cutting and reattaching pieces of a simpler tile (this is how M.C. Escher-style tessellations are made)

Reminder — Which Symmetries Count

You must use at least two of the three symmetry types below. Read each description carefully to make sure your design genuinely uses both, not just one repeated in different directions.

  • Translation symmetry: The entire pattern shifts by a fixed vector and maps onto itself. All true tessellations have this. It counts as one of your two required symmetries only if you can identify and state the specific translation vector.
  • Reflection symmetry: The entire tessellation pattern — not just one tile — maps onto itself when reflected across a line running through the pattern. The line of symmetry must pass through the repeating arrangement of tiles, not just through a single tile.
  • Rotational symmetry: The entire pattern maps onto itself when rotated by a specific angle about a specific center point in the pattern. State the center and angle.


How to Create Your Tessellation

Option A — Paper and pencil or markers.   Use graph paper or plain paper. Draw your tile template first, then trace it repeatedly to build the tessellation. Use a ruler for straight edges. Color or shade alternate tiles if it helps make the pattern clearer.

Option B — GeoGebra (free at geogebra.org).   Use the polygon tool to construct your tile, then use the Move tool or the Reflect / Rotate / Translate tools in the Transform menu to copy it across the plane. The Geometry or Classic app works well for this. Export or screenshot your finished design.

Option C — Desmos Geometry (free at desmos.com/geometry).   Use the polygon tool to draw your tile. Use the transformation tools (Reflect, Rotate, Translate) to generate additional copies. Screenshot your finished design.

Before You Move to Part 2 — Check Your Work

  • Can you point to at least 12 complete tiles in your design?
  • Are there any gaps or overlaps? If so, adjust your tile shape before continuing.
  • Can you identify at least two different symmetry types present in your finished pattern? If you can only find one, you may need to choose a different shape or arrangement.
📋 PART 2 — WRITTEN ANALYSIS

Answer each question below in complete sentences. Your responses should be specific to your tessellation — generic answers that could apply to any design will not receive full credit. Use correct mathematical vocabulary from this lesson.

Section A — Your Shape(s)

Question 1. What shape or shapes did you use as the tile(s) in your tessellation? Describe each shape precisely — include the number of sides, whether it is regular or irregular, and any other relevant geometric properties (such as side lengths or angle measures if you know them).

Question 2. Why does the shape (or combination of shapes) you chose tessellate the plane? Explain using angle measures. Specifically, what is the sum of the angles meeting at any interior vertex point of your tessellation, and why does that sum allow the tiles to fit together without gaps or overlaps?

Question 3. What symmetries does a single tile have on its own — before you consider the full repeated pattern? State the number of lines of reflection symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry for each tile shape you used. If a tile has no reflection symmetry or only trivial rotational symmetry (order 1), state that explicitly.



Section B — Symmetries of Your Tessellation

Question 4. Identify the translation symmetry (or symmetries) in your tessellation. Describe at least one translation vector — give its direction and the distance it shifts the pattern. Explain how you know it is a symmetry: what does the pattern look like before and after the translation?

Question 5. Identify the second type of symmetry present in your tessellation (reflection or rotational — whichever applies). Be specific:

  • If reflection symmetry: describe where a line of symmetry runs through the pattern. Does it pass through tiles, along edges between tiles, or through vertex points? Is there more than one such line?
  • If rotational symmetry: identify a center point of rotation in the pattern (for example, "the center of every tile" or "every vertex where six tiles meet"). What is the angle of rotation, and what is the order?

Question 6. If your tessellation has a third type of symmetry beyond the two you described above, identify it here using the same level of detail as Questions 4 and 5. If your tessellation has only the two required symmetries, explain why the third type is not present — what property of your tile or arrangement prevents it?



Section C — Reflection and Connection

Question 7. How did the symmetries of your individual tile shape influence or guide the symmetries of the full tessellation? In other words, did the reflection or rotational symmetries that a single tile has on its own show up in the repeated pattern as well, or did new symmetries emerge only because of the way the tiles were arranged together? Explain with a specific example from your design.

Question 8. Every tessellation has translation symmetry. Does having translation symmetry mean a tessellation automatically has reflection or rotational symmetry as well? Use your tessellation as evidence to support your answer. (There is no single "right" answer here — your tessellation's specific symmetries should guide your response.)

 

Submission Checklist

Before submitting, confirm that your work includes each of the following:

  • A clear image or file of your completed tessellation showing at least 12 complete tiles.
  • Answers to all 8 questions in Part 2, written in complete sentences.
  • At least two different symmetry types identified and described specifically in your written responses.
  • Correct use of vocabulary:line of symmetry,order of rotational symmetry,angle of rotational symmetry,translation vector, andtessellation.
  • Responses that are specific to your design — not generic descriptions that could apply to any tessellation.

 

Scoring Note

Part 1 (the tessellation) and Part 2 (the written analysis) are both required and will be scored separately. A visually impressive tessellation with weak written analysis, or strong written analysis describing a tessellation that does not meet the requirements, will each result in a reduced score. Both parts should reflect careful mathematical thinking.
