📚 VOCABULARY

Congruent: Figures that have the same size and shape, with corresponding angles and sides equal.

Dilation: A transformation that changes the size of a figure while maintaining its shape; it produces a similar figure based on a scale factor.

Image: The resulting figure after one or more transformations have been applied to the original figure.

Non-Rigid Motion: A transformation, such as a dilation, that changes the size of a figure and does not preserve distance; it is used to justify similarity.

Pre-Image: The original figure before any transformations have been applied.

Reflection: A rigid transformation that flips a figure over a line, creating a mirror image.

Rigid Motion: A transformation that preserves size and shape, such as a reflection, rotation, or translation; it is used to justify congruence.

Rotation: A rigid transformation that turns a figure around a fixed point by a specific angle and direction.

Sequence of Transformations: A set of two or more transformations applied in a specific order to a figure.

Similar: Figures that have the same shape but not necessarily the same size; corresponding angles are equal, and side lengths are proportional.

Transformation: A change in the position, size, or orientation of a figure on a plane.

Translation: A rigid transformation that slides a figure from one location to another without changing its orientation or size.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore sequences of transformations in a plane, including reflections, rotations, translations, and dilations. Transformations are movements or changes applied to geometric figures that affect their position, size, or orientation. Sometimes we apply just one transformation, and other times we apply two or more in a sequence. Understanding how these transformations work individually and together will help you recognize when figures are congruent or similar and how to justify that relationship using geometric reasoning.

Review of Transformations

Translation Reflection Rotation Dilation
A translation slides a figure without turning or flipping it. It preserves both the size and the orientation of the shape. A reflection flips the figure over a line, creating a mirror image. Reflected figures maintain their size and shape, but their orientation is reversed. A rotation turns a figure around a fixed point, usually by 90°, 180°, or 270°, either clockwise or counterclockwise. The shape and size remain unchanged. A dilation resizes a figure by expanding or shrinking it from a fixed center point based on a scale factor. This transformation does not preserve size but does preserve shape.
  • The first three—translation, reflection, and rotation—are called rigid motions because they preserve both size and shape.
  • The fourth, dilation, is a non-rigid motion because it changes the size of the figure while maintaining the shape.



Identifying a Sequence of Transformations

Sometimes a figure undergoes more than one transformation. For example, a triangle might first be reflected over a line, then rotated 90° clockwise around a point. This is called a sequence of transformations. To identify the sequence:

  1. Look at the pre-image and the image.

  2. Compare their positions, orientations, and sizes.

  3. Ask yourself: Did the figure flip? Rotate? Slide? Resize?

  4. Determine the correct order in which these changes occurred.



Example 1

Triangle ABC was transformed to create triangle A'B'C'. 

Identify the sequence of transformations.

A. The figure was translated left, then rotated \(180^\circ\).

B. The figure was translated down and then dilated by a factor of \(\frac{1}{2}\).

C. The figure was reflected over a horizontal line, then rotated \(90^\circ\) clockwise.

D. The figure was reflected over a vertical line, then translated down.

Solution:

  • We notice that in the image, it appears that vertices A and C switched places in the pre-image.
  • This helps us to identify that the figure was reflected over a vertical line to create a mirror image of it to the right.
  • Next, we notice the final image is below the reflected image.
  • So, the next transformation would be a translation down.

The answer is option D: Reflected and translated down.



Example 2

Quadrilateral GHIJ was transformed to create quadrilateral G'H'I'J'.

Identify the sequence of transformations.

A. The figure was translated right, then reflected over a horizontal line.

B. The figure was rotated and then dilated by a factor of \(\frac{1}{2}\).

C. The figure was rotated \(90^\circ\) clockwise, then translated down.

D. The figure was reflected over a vertical line, then translated down.

Solution:

  • We notice that in the preimage, vertices H and G creating line segment \(\overline{HG}\) were on the left of the preimage and are now at the top of the image.
  • This helps us to identify that the figure was rotated. We can also identify that it was rotated 90 degrees, potentially around the vertex I as shown below.
  • Next, we notice the final image is below the rotated image.
  • So, the next transformation would be a translation down.

The answer is option C: The figure was rotated \(90^\circ\) clockwise, then translated down.



Congruence and Similarity from Sequences

To determine whether a sequence of transformations maps a figure onto a congruent figure or a similar figure:

  • If the sequence includes only rigid motions (translation, rotation, reflection), the figures are congruent.

  • If the sequence includes a dilation (with or without rigid motions), the figures are similar.

Justifying Congruence:
When figures are congruent, we can justify this by showing that a series of rigid motions maps the pre-image exactly onto the image.

  • For example, “Figure A is congruent to Figure B because it can be reflected over line m and then translated 4 units to the right.”


Justifying Similarity:
When figures are similar, we use non-rigid motion (usually dilation) along with any rigid motions needed.

  • For example, “Figure A is similar to Figure B because a dilation with a scale factor of 1.5 centered at point C followed by a rotation of 180° maps A onto B.”



Example 3

Triangles \(\bigtriangleup MNO\) and \(\bigtriangleup PQO\) are shown.

Questions:
Determine whether the two triangles shown are congruent or similar.

  • Identify and describe the transformations that map \(\bigtriangleup MNO\) onto \(\bigtriangleup PQO\).

  • Classify each transformation as a rigid motion or a non-rigid motion.

  • Based on your findings, justify whether the figures are congruent or similar.

Solution:

  • We see that \(\bigtriangleup PQO\) appears to be reflected over a horizontal line below \(\bigtriangleup MNO\) and dilated by a factor less than 1 to shrink it.
  • We could map figure \(\bigtriangleup MNO\) onto \(\bigtriangleup PQO\) through those transformations.
  • We know that a reflection is a rigid motion that creates congruent figures.
  • However, a dilation is a non-rigid motion that creates similar figures.
  • Therefore, we know that \(\bigtriangleup MNO\) and \(\bigtriangleup PQO\) are similar by a sequence of rigid and non-rigid motions.



Example 4

Quadrilaterals RSTU and VWXY are shown.

Questions:
Determine whether the two quadrilaterals shown are congruent or similar.

  • Identify and describe the transformations that map quadrilateral RSTU onto quadrilateral VWXY.

  • Classify each transformation as a rigid motion or a non-rigid motion.

  • Based on your findings, justify whether the figures are congruent or similar.

Solution:

  • We see that VWXY appears to be rotated from RSTU and then shifted sideways.
  • We could map figure RSTU onto VWXY through those transformations.
  • We know that a rotation and a translation are rigid motions that create congruent figures.
  • Therefore, we know that quadrilaterals RSTU and VWXY are congruent by a sequence of rigid motions.



Conclusion

Today, you explored how geometric figures move and change through transformations in a plane. You reviewed the four basic transformations and learned how to identify a sequence of these changes. By studying how transformations affect size and shape, you can determine whether two figures are congruent or similar. Understanding rigid and non-rigid motions gives you the tools to justify these relationships and describe geometric reasoning with precision. In future lessons, you’ll apply these skills to more complex figures and real-world design problems where transformation sequences play a key role.
