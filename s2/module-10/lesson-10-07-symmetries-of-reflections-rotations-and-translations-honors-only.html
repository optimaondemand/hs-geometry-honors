📚 VOCABULARY

Angle of Rotational Symmetry: The smallest positive angle through which a figure can be rotated about a center point and map exactly onto itself; for a figure of order \(n\), this angle equals \(\dfrac{360^\circ}{n}\).

Center of Rotational Symmetry: The fixed point about which a figure is rotated when it maps onto itself; also called the center of symmetry for rotational symmetry.

Fundamental Domain: A minimal region of a tessellation that, when copied by a translation, generates the entire pattern without gaps or overlaps.

Line of Symmetry: A line that divides a figure into two congruent halves that are mirror images of each other; reflecting the figure across this line maps it exactly onto itself. Also called an axis of symmetry or line of reflection symmetry.

Order of Rotational Symmetry: The number of distinct rotations (including the \(360^\circ \) rotation) that map a figure onto itself; a figure with no rotational symmetry other than the full rotation has order \(1\).

Reflection Symmetry: A symmetry of a figure in which a reflection across a specific line maps the figure exactly onto itself; a figure may have zero, one, or multiple lines of reflection symmetry.

Rotational Symmetry: A symmetry of a figure in which a rotation by some angle less than \(360^\circ \) about a fixed center point maps the figure exactly onto itself.

Symmetry: A transformation of a geometric figure that maps the figure exactly onto itself, preserving all distances and angles; the figure looks unchanged after the transformation is applied.

Tessellation: An arrangement of one or more geometric shapes that covers the entire plane without gaps or overlaps; also called a tiling of the plane.

Translation Symmetry: A symmetry of a figure or pattern in which a translation by a specific vector maps the figure or pattern exactly onto itself; individual finite figures do not have translation symmetry, but infinite repeating patterns such as tessellations do.

Translation Vector: A vector  that describes the direction and distance of a translation; in a tessellation, the translation vector defines how far and in what direction the pattern repeats.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

You have already studied reflections, rotations, translations, and dilations as individual transformations and as sequences of transformations in the coordinate plane. In this lesson, you will turn that knowledge toward a new question: what happens when a transformation maps a figure exactly back onto itself? When a figure looks unchanged after a transformation is applied, that transformation is called a symmetry of the figure. You will investigate three types of symmetry — reflection symmetry, rotational symmetry, and translation symmetry — and learn to identify, count, and describe each one precisely. For rotational symmetry, you will also learn to state its order, a number that captures how many times the figure repeats as it completes a full turn. Finally, you will explore how translation symmetry is the mathematical engine behind tessellations — the repeating patterns that tile the plane without gaps or overlaps. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to fully describe the symmetry profile of any geometric figure and explain why tessellations are inherently translational.

What Is a Symmetry?

A symmetry of a geometric figure is a transformation — a reflection, rotation, or translation — that maps the figure exactly onto itself. "Exactly onto itself" means every point of the figure ends up on another point of the figure (or back on the same point), and the overall shape, size, and position of the figure appear completely unchanged.

This is more precise than just saying a figure "looks the same." A symmetry is a specific, named transformation: a reflection across a particular line, a rotation by a particular angle about a particular center, or a translation by a particular vector. When we say a figure has a symmetry, we mean we can carry out that exact transformation and the figure is indistinguishable from how it started.

Key Idea — A Symmetry Must Be a Specific Transformation

It is not enough to say "the figure has reflection symmetry." You must identify which line. It is not enough to say "the figure has rotational symmetry." You must identify which angle and which center. Describing a symmetry precisely always includes naming the transformation completely.



Symmetries of Reflection

A figure has a symmetry of reflection (also called reflection symmetry or line symmetry) if there exists a line — called the line of symmetry or axis of symmetry — such that reflecting the entire figure across that line maps the figure exactly onto itself. Each line of symmetry folds the figure perfectly in half: the two halves are mirror images of each other, and every point on one half has a corresponding point directly across the line on the other half.

A figure may have zero, one, or several lines of symmetry. To find all lines of symmetry for a figure, ask: across how many different lines can I "fold" this figure so that both halves match perfectly? Each such fold line is a distinct reflection symmetry.

How to Identify Lines of Symmetry

  1. Look for lines that divide the figure into two congruent, mirror-image halves. Common candidates are lines through the center, lines connecting midpoints of opposite sides, and lines through vertices.
  2. For each candidate line, check that every point of the figure has a mirror image that is also on the figure and at the same distance from the line.
  3. Count the total number of lines that pass this check. That number is how many reflection symmetries the figure has.


Regular polygons and reflection symmetry.  

A regular polygon with \( n \) sides has exactly \( n \) lines of reflection symmetry. For a polygon with an even number of sides, the lines of symmetry come in two types: lines connecting opposite vertices, and lines connecting midpoints of opposite sides. For a polygon with an odd number of sides, each line of symmetry passes through one vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

Reflection of Symmetries of Regular Polygons

Equilateral triangle \( (n = 3) \):   \( 3 \) lines of symmetry, each through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

Square \( (n = 4) \):   \( 4 \) lines of symmetry — \( 2 \) through opposite vertices, \( 2 \) through midpoints of opposite sides.

Regular pentagon \( (n = 5) \):   \( 5 \) lines of symmetry, each through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

Regular hexagon \( (n = 6) \):   \( 6 \) lines of symmetry — \( 3 \) through opposite vertices, \( 3 \) through midpoints of opposite sides.

A regular \( n \)-gon always has exactly \( n \) lines of reflection symmetry.

Example

How many lines of reflection symmetry does a regular octagon have? Describe the two types.

Show Solution

Step 1: A regular octagon has \( n = 8 \) sides, so it has exactly \( 8 \) lines of reflection symmetry.

Step 2: Since \( n \) is even, the symmetry lines come in two types.

Type 1: Lines connecting opposite vertices. There are \( \dfrac{8}{2} = 4 \) pairs of opposite vertices, giving \( 4 \) such lines.

Type 2: Lines connecting midpoints of opposite sides. There are \( \dfrac{8}{2} = 4 \) pairs of opposite sides, giving \( 4 \) such lines.

Step 3: Total: \( 4 + 4 = 8 \) lines of reflection symmetry. ✓

Answer: A regular octagon has 8 lines of reflection symmetry — 4 through pairs of opposite vertices and 4 through midpoints of opposite sides.



Symmetries of Rotation and Order

A figure has a symmetry of rotation if there exists a center point and an angle of rotation (greater than \( 0° \) and less than or equal to \( 360° \)) such that rotating the figure by that angle about that center maps the figure exactly onto itself. The center is called the center of rotational symmetry, and the smallest such positive angle is called the angle of rotational symmetry.

The order of rotational symmetry is the number of distinct rotations — including the full \( 360° \) rotation — that map the figure onto itself. A figure with order \( n \) maps onto itself at angles of \( \dfrac{360°}{n} \), \( \dfrac{2 \cdot 360°}{n} \), \( \dfrac{3 \cdot 360°}{n} \), and so on, up through \( 360° \). Every figure technically maps onto itself under a \( 360° \) rotation (the identity), so if that is the only rotation that works, the figure has rotational symmetry of order \( 1 \) — which is conventionally interpreted as having no non-trivial rotational symmetry.

Key Formula — Angle and Order of Rotational Symmetry

If a figure has rotational symmetry of order \( n \), then the smallest angle of rotation that maps it onto itself is:

\( \text{Angle} = \dfrac{360°}{n} \)

Conversely, if you know the smallest angle \( \theta \) that works, the order is \( n = \dfrac{360°}{\theta} \).

The figure maps onto itself at every multiple of this angle: \( \theta,\ 2\theta,\ 3\theta,\ \ldots,\ n\theta = 360° \).


For regular polygons, the order of rotational symmetry always equals the number of sides. The center of rotational symmetry is the center of the polygon.

Figure Order of Rotational Symmetry Angle of Rotational Symmetry All Angles That Work
Equilateral Triangle \(3\) \(\dfrac{360}{3} = 120^\circ \) \( 120^\circ, 240^\circ, 360^\circ \)
Square \(4\) \(\dfrac{360}{4} = 90^\circ \) \( 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ, 360^\circ \)
Regular Pentagon \(5\) \(\dfrac{360}{5} = 72^\circ \) \( 72^\circ, 144^\circ, 216^\circ, 288^\circ, 360^\circ \)
Regular Hexagon \(6\) \(\dfrac{360}{6} = 60^\circ \) \( 60^\circ, 120^\circ, 180^\circ, 240^\circ, 300^\circ, 360^\circ \)
Regular \(n\)-gon \(n\) \(\dfrac{360}{n}^\circ \) All multiples of \(\dfrac{360}{n}^\circ \) up to \(360^\circ \)

 

Example

A regular decagon (10-sided polygon) is centered at the origin. State its order of rotational symmetry, the angle of rotational symmetry, and list all angles that map it onto itself.

Show Solution

Step 1: A regular decagon has \( n = 10 \) sides, so its order of rotational symmetry is \( \mathbf{10} \).

Step 2: The angle of rotational symmetry is \( \dfrac{360°}{10} = 36° \).

Step 3: The decagon maps onto itself at every multiple of \( 36° \):

\( 36°,\ 72°,\ 108°,\ 144°,\ 180°,\ 216°,\ 252°,\ 288°,\ 324°,\ 360° \)

There are exactly \( 10 \) such angles, confirming the order is \( 10 \). ✓

Answer: Order \( = 10 \); angle of rotational symmetry \( = 36° \); the figure maps onto itself at \( 36°, 72°, 108°, 144°, 180°, 216°, 252°, 288°, 324°, \) and \( 360° \).

Non-Regular Figures and Rotational Symmetry

Not all figures with rotational symmetry are regular polygons. A figure can have rotational symmetry of order \( 2 \) (also called point symmetry or 180° symmetry) without being a regular polygon — for example, a parallelogram that is not a rectangle. A circle has infinitely many rotational symmetries. Letters like S and Z have order-2 rotational symmetry. To find the order for any figure, find the smallest angle that maps it onto itself, then compute \( n = \dfrac{360°}{\theta} \).



Symmetries of Translation

A symmetry of translation is different in character from reflection and rotational symmetry. A single, finite geometric figure — such as a triangle or hexagon drawn on paper — cannot have translational symmetry, because sliding any finite shape in any direction will move it to a new position where it no longer coincides with itself. Translation symmetry belongs to infinite repeating patterns: configurations that extend without end in at least one direction, repeating the same motif over and over.

A pattern has a symmetry of translation if there exists a translation vector \( \langle a, b \rangle \) such that translating the entire infinite pattern by that vector maps the pattern exactly onto itself. The pattern looks completely unchanged after the shift. A pattern may have more than one such translation vector — for example, a two-dimensional repeating pattern (like wallpaper) typically has two independent translation vectors that together generate the full lattice of repetitions.

What Makes a Translation a Symmetry of a Pattern

A translation by vector \( \langle a, b \rangle \) is a symmetry of a pattern when every point in the pattern, after being shifted by \( \langle a, b \rangle \), lands on another point that is also in the pattern — and this is true for every point simultaneously, not just some. The translation must map the entire pattern onto itself perfectly.

Note that any integer multiple of a symmetry translation vector is also a symmetry translation vector. If \( \langle a, b \rangle \) is a translation symmetry, then so is \( \langle 2a, 2b \rangle \), \( \langle 3a, 3b \rangle \), and so on.



Tessellations and Translation Symmetry

A tessellation (or tiling of the plane) is an arrangement of one or more geometric shapes — called tiles — that covers the entire infinite plane without gaps or overlaps. You have likely seen tessellations in floor tiles, honeycomb patterns, and decorative artwork.

Every tessellation of the plane possesses at least one symmetry of translation — in fact, it always has infinitely many, because every integer multiple of any translation vector also works. The translation vector of a tessellation describes how far and in which direction you must shift the entire pattern to land back on an identical copy of itself. Because the pattern repeats periodically, you can always find such a vector.

The smallest region of a tessellation that can be used to generate the entire pattern by translation alone is called the fundamental domain (or fundamental tile). Translating the fundamental domain by all integer combinations of the two generating translation vectors produces the complete tessellation.

The Connection Between Tessellations and Translation Symmetry

A tessellation of the plane is precisely a pattern that has translation symmetry. The two ideas define each other:

— A tessellation must have translation symmetry, because the repeating nature of the pattern guarantees that a shift of exactly one tile-length in the repeat direction maps the pattern onto itself.

— Any infinite pattern with translation symmetry tiles the plane in at least one direction — it is, in that sense, a tessellation.

The translation vectors of a tessellation are not arbitrary; they are determined by the size and arrangement of the tiles. Identifying these vectors is the same as identifying the period of the pattern.

Example

A tessellation of the plane is made by repeating a rectangle that is \( 4 \) units wide and \( 3 \) units tall, arranged in a grid pattern with no gaps. Identify two independent translation vectors that are symmetries of this tessellation.

Show Solution

Step 1: The rectangle tiles have width \( 4 \) and height \( 3 \). Because the pattern repeats every \( 4 \) units horizontally, shifting the entire pattern \( 4 \) units to the right maps every tile to the next tile in the same row.

Translation vector 1: \( \langle 4, 0 \rangle \)

Step 2: Because the pattern also repeats every \( 3 \) units vertically, shifting the entire pattern \( 3 \) units upward maps every tile to the tile directly above it in the same column.

Translation vector 2: \( \langle 0, 3 \rangle \)

Step 3: These two vectors are independent (they point in different directions and neither is a scalar multiple of the other). Any integer combination \( m\langle 4, 0 \rangle + n\langle 0, 3 \rangle = \langle 4m, 3n \rangle \) is also a translation symmetry of the tessellation.

Answer: Two independent translation symmetry vectors are \( \langle 4, 0 \rangle \) (shift right by one tile-width) and \( \langle 0, 3 \rangle \) (shift up by one tile-height). All integer combinations of these vectors are also translation symmetries.

Translation Symmetry in a Tessellation

A tessellation of the plane is created using equilateral triangles with side length \( 2 \), arranged so that the rows of triangles alternate pointing up and pointing down, forming the standard triangular tiling. The vertices of the tiling fall on a triangular lattice.

(a) Identify one horizontal translation vector that is a symmetry of this tessellation.

(b) Identify one non-horizontal translation vector that is a symmetry of this tessellation.

(c) Explain why every integer multiple of a translation symmetry vector is also a translation symmetry of the tessellation.

Show Solution

Part (a): In the standard triangular tiling with side length \( 2 \), two adjacent triangles together span a horizontal distance of \( 2 \) (one full side length). The tiling repeats horizontally every \( 2 \) units, so a horizontal translation by \( \langle 2, 0 \rangle \) maps every triangle to an identical triangle in the same row.

Horizontal translation vector: \( \langle 2, 0 \rangle \)

Part (b): In the triangular lattice, moving from one vertex to the nearest vertex in the upper-right direction traces one edge of an equilateral triangle. For a triangle with side length \( 2 \), this vector points at \( 60° \) from horizontal with magnitude \( 2 \). Its components are \( \langle 2\cos 60°, 2\sin 60° \rangle = \langle 1, \sqrt{3} \rangle \). Translating the entire tessellation by \( \langle 1, \sqrt{3} \rangle \) maps every vertex to the next vertex up-right and maps the entire pattern onto itself.

Non-horizontal translation vector: \( \langle 1, \sqrt{3} \rangle \)

Part (c): If \( \langle a, b \rangle \) is a translation symmetry — meaning the pattern looks identical after shifting by \( \langle a, b \rangle \) — then shifting by \( \langle a, b \rangle \) again produces the same result again, because the pattern still looks the same after the first shift. Repeating this \( m \) times gives a total shift of \( \langle ma, mb \rangle \), and at each step the pattern maps onto itself. Therefore \( \langle ma, mb \rangle \) is also a translation symmetry for any positive integer \( m \). The same reasoning applies for shifts in the opposite direction (negative integers).

(a) \( \langle 2, 0 \rangle \) (shift right by one side length).

(b) \( \langle 1, \sqrt{3} \rangle \) (shift in the direction of a \( 60° \) edge).

(c) Applying a translation symmetry repeatedly is the same as applying a single larger translation. Each repetition keeps the pattern mapped onto itself, so all integer multiples are also symmetries.



Relationship Between Reflection and Rotation Symmetry

Consider a regular polygon with \( n \) sides.

(a) A regular hexagon has \( 6 \) lines of reflection symmetry and rotational symmetry of order \( 6 \). A square has \( 4 \) lines of reflection symmetry and rotational symmetry of order \( 4 \). Make a conjecture: for a regular \( n \)-gon, how do the number of lines of reflection symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry compare?

(b) Two lines of symmetry of a regular hexagon pass through opposite vertices and intersect at the center at an angle of \( 60° \). What rotation does the composition of two reflections across intersecting lines produce, and what is the angle of that rotation? (Recall from earlier lessons that the composition of two reflections across lines that intersect at angle \( \theta \) is a rotation by \( 2\theta \).)

(c) Use part (b) to explain why a figure with two lines of reflection symmetry that intersect at the center always has rotational symmetry as well. What is the order of that rotational symmetry if the two lines are separated by angle \( \theta \)?

Show Solution

Part (a) — Conjecture: In every case — equilateral triangle \( (3, 3) \), square \( (4, 4) \), pentagon \( (5, 5) \), hexagon \( (6, 6) \) — the number of lines of reflection symmetry equals the order of rotational symmetry. The conjecture is: for a regular \( n \)-gon, the number of lines of reflection symmetry equals the order of rotational symmetry, and both equal \( n \).

Part (b): The two lines of symmetry of the regular hexagon that pass through opposite vertices are separated by an angle of \( 60° \) at the center (since \( \dfrac{360°}{6} \div 2 = 30° \)... let's be precise: adjacent lines of symmetry of the hexagon are separated by \( \dfrac{180°}{6} = 30° \). So two lines separated by \( 30° \) produce a rotation of \( 2 \times 30° = 60° \) about the center. This \( 60° \) rotation is exactly the angle of rotational symmetry of the hexagon — confirming the connection.

Part (c): If a figure has two lines of reflection symmetry intersecting at the center at angle \( \theta \), then the composition of the two reflections is a rotation by \( 2\theta \) about the center. Since each reflection maps the figure onto itself, their composition (the rotation) also maps the figure onto itself — so \( 2\theta \) is an angle of rotational symmetry. The order is \( \dfrac{360°}{2\theta} \). This shows that whenever a figure has two or more lines of reflection symmetry through a common center, it is guaranteed to also have rotational symmetry — the reflections generate the rotations automatically.

(a) Conjecture: for a regular \( n \)-gon, the number of lines of reflection symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry are both equal to \( n \).

(b) Adjacent lines of symmetry of the regular hexagon meet at \( 30° \), producing a rotation of \( 2 \times 30° = 60° \) — the angle of rotational symmetry of the hexagon.

(c) Two lines of symmetry meeting at angle \( \theta \) produce a rotation of \( 2\theta \). Because each reflection is a symmetry, so is their composition. The rotational symmetry has order \( \dfrac{360°}{2\theta} \). Therefore, any figure with two intersecting lines of reflection symmetry automatically has rotational symmetry.



Conclusion

In this lesson, you extended your knowledge of transformations to investigate when a transformation maps a figure exactly onto itself — what mathematicians call a symmetry. You learned to identify and count lines of reflection symmetry for geometric figures, connecting the result to the structure of regular polygons where a regular \( n \)-gon always has exactly \( n \) lines of symmetry. For rotational symmetry, you learned to determine the order — the number of distinct rotations that map the figure onto itself — and the angle of rotational symmetry, given by the formula \( \dfrac{360°}{n} \). You also saw that rotational symmetry of order \( 1 \) means no non-trivial rotational symmetry exists, and that many common figures, such as non-square rectangles, have order \( 2 \). Translation symmetry, unlike the other two, applies only to infinite repeating patterns: a single finite figure cannot slide onto itself, but an infinite tessellation of the plane always possesses translation symmetry because its repeating structure guarantees that a shift of exactly one period maps the pattern onto itself. The connection between tessellations and translation symmetry is precise and fundamental — a pattern tiles the plane if and only if it has translation symmetry, and the translation vectors describe the repeat structure of the tiling. In the honors extension, you also saw a deeper connection: two intersecting lines of reflection symmetry always generate a rotational symmetry, which is why regular polygons have the same number of reflection lines and rotational orders. These three types of symmetry together give you a complete framework for analyzing the self-mapping structure of any geometric figure or pattern.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What does it mean for a geometric figure to be symmetric under a transformation, and how does each type of symmetry — reflection, rotation, and translation — describe a different kind of self-mapping?

2. How does the order of a rotational symmetry of a figure relate to the structure and regularity of that figure, and what can the order tell you about the figure's other symmetries?

3. Why do tessellations of the plane necessarily possess symmetries of translation, and how does the concept of translational symmetry connect the geometry of a single tile to the infinite repeating pattern?
