📚 VOCABULARY

Algebraic Representation: A way to describe a transformation using equations that show how the coordinates of a figure change.

Congruent: Figures that have the same shape and size, with corresponding sides and angles equal.

Coordinate Plane: A two-dimensional surface formed by a horizontal line (x-axis) and a vertical line (y-axis) that intersect at the origin.

Image: The new figure that results after a transformation has been applied to a pre-image.

Origin: The point (0, 0) where the x-axis and y-axis meet in the coordinate plane.

Pre-Image: The original figure before any transformation has been applied.

Reflection: A transformation that flips a figure across a line to create a mirror image.

Rotation: A transformation that turns a figure around a fixed point by a certain angle and direction.

Transformation: A change in the position, size, or orientation of a figure.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore how to describe and represent reflections and rotations algebraically in the coordinate plane. You will learn how the movement of figures on the plane can be captured using simple coordinate rules. We will look at how to match geometric descriptions to algebraic representations, and how to work from a pre-image and an image to determine a transformation rule. Throughout the lesson, examples and practice problems will help you become comfortable with identifying, describing, and graphing these transformations.


Reflection Rules and Algebraic Representations

Reflection Across the x-axis:

\((x,y)\rightarrow (x,-y)\)

Reflection Across the y-axis

\((x,y)\rightarrow (-x,y)\)

Representing a Reflection Algebraically:

When given a pre-image and image, compare the coordinates:

  • If only the y-values change sign, it is a reflection over the x-axis.

  • If only the x-values change sign, it is a reflection over the y-axis.

  • If x and y swap places, it is a reflection over \(y=x\).

  • If x and y swap and both change sign, it is a reflection over \(y=-x\).



Example 1

The quadrilateral ABCD is transformed to create quadrilateral A'B'C'D' as shown.

Describe the transformation geometrically.

Represent the transformation algebraically.

Solution:

  • To describe geometrically, you need to look at how to map ABCD onto A'B'C'D'.
  • If it is a reflection, what are you reflecting over?
  • Once we identify the transformation, then we can look at how to represent it algebraically.
  • The quadrilateral is a reflection since the image is upside down from the original.
  • It was reflected over the x-axis as we can think of the x-axis as the mirror for the images.
  • This makes the algebraic representation of that be \((x,y)\rightarrow (x,-y)\).

Answer: Quadrilateral ABCD was reflected over the x-axis to create quadrilateral A'B'C'D'. Therefore, \((x,y)\rightarrow (x,-y)\).



Example 2

Triangle XYZ has the following vertices \(X(2,5)\), \(Y(4,7)\), and \(Z(6,2)\).

The triangle is reflected over the y-axis.

Find the coordinates of the image \(\bigtriangleup X'Y'Z'\).

Solution:

  • Since we are reflecting over the y-axis, we will use the algebraic rule \((x,y)\rightarrow (-x,y)\).
  • We apply the rule to each vertex of \(\bigtriangleup XYZ\) to find the coordinates of each vertex in \(\bigtriangleup X'Y'Z'\).
  • So, now let's work through applying the rule to each vertex.
    • \(X(2,5) \rightarrow X'(-2,5)\)
    • \(Y(4,7) \rightarrow Y'(-4,7)\)
    • \(Z(6,2) \rightarrow Z'(-6,2)\)

Answer: The coordinates of \(\bigtriangleup X'Y'Z'\) are \(X'(-2,5)\), \(Y'(-4,7)\), and \(Z'(-6,2)\).



Example 3

Quadrilateral ABCD has the following vertices \(A(1,3)\), \(B(4,2)\), \(C(5,6)\), and \(D(2,7)\) as shown.

Draw the image of the quadrilateral reflected over the line \(y=x\).

Solution:

  • To draw the reflection, it will be helpful first if we find the coordinates of the vertices for the reflection.
  • To find the coordinates of the reflection, we will apply the rule \((x,y)\rightarrow (y,x)\).
  • So, we begin finding the coordinates of the vertices of the reflected quadrilateral.
    • \(A(1,3) \rightarrow (3,1)\)
    • \(B(4,2) \rightarrow (2,4)\)
    • \(C(5,6) \rightarrow (6,5)\)
    • \(D(2,7) \rightarrow (7,2)\)
  • Now that we have the coordinates of the vertices for the reflected quadrilaterals, we can graph those and connect them with a polygon.

The quadrilateral EFGH represents the reflection of ABCD over the line \(y=x\).



Rotation Rules and Algebraic Representations (around the origin)

Rotation of \(90^\circ \) counterclockwise

(Also \(270^\circ \) clockwise)

\((x,y)\rightarrow (-y,x)\)

  • negate the y coordinate and move it to the x position, then keep x the same and move it to the y position.

Rotation of \(180^\circ \)

(A rotation of 180 degrees counterclockwise is the same as 180 degrees clockwise)

\((x,y)\rightarrow (-x,-y)\)

  • negate both x and y and leave them in their current positions

Rotation of \(270^\circ \) counterclockwise

(Also \(90^\circ \) clockwise)

\((x,y)\rightarrow (y,-x)\)

  • negate the x value and move it to the y position, keep y the same and move it to the x position

Representing a Rotation Algebraically:

When given a pre-image and image, examine the coordinate changes:

  • If the x and y values swap and one value changes sign, it’s a 90° or 270° rotation.

  • If both x and y values change signs, it’s a 180° rotation.


Example 1

The triangle ABC is transformed to create triangle A'B'C' as shown.

Describe the transformation geometrically.

Represent the transformation algebraically.

Solution:

  • To describe geometrically, you need to look at how to map ABC onto A'B'C'.
  • We can look at the rotation 2 ways as shown here:
    • We are either rotation 90 degrees counterclockwise or 270 clockwise.
    • From the chart, we know these two produce the same algebraic representation.
  • The triangle is rotated 90 degrees counterclockwise since it rotated one quadrant in a counterclockwise direction.
  • This makes the algebraic representation of that be \((x,y)\rightarrow (-y,x)\).

Answer: Triangle ABC was rotated 90 degrees counterclockwise around the origin to create triangle A'B'C'. Therefore, \((x,y)\rightarrow (-y,x)\).



Example 2

Triangle EFG has the following vertices \(E(-10,-4)\), \(F(-5,5)\), and \(G(1,0)\).

The triangle is rotated 270 degrees counterclockwise around the origin.

Find the coordinates of the image \(\bigtriangleup E'F'G'\).

Solution:

  • Since we are rotating 270 degrees counterclockwise around the origin, we will use the algebraic rule \((x,y)\rightarrow (y,-x)\).
  • We apply the rule to each vertex of \(\bigtriangleup EFG\) to find the coordinates of each vertex in \(\bigtriangleup E'F'G'\).
  • So, now let's work through applying the rule to each vertex.
    • \(E(-10,-4) \rightarrow E'(-4,10)\) - We keep y as it is, but it comes first. We negate x and make it the second coordinate.
    • \(F(-5,5) \rightarrow F'(5,5)\)
    • \(G(1,0) \rightarrow G'(0,-1)\)

Answer: The coordinates of \(\bigtriangleup E'F'G'\) are \(E'(-4,10)\), \(F'(5,5)\), and \(G'(0,-1)\).



Example 3

Quadrilateral ABCD has the following vertices \(A(-8,1)\), \(B(0,5)\), \(C(-2,9)\), and \(D(-10,7)\) as shown.

Draw the image of the quadrilateral rotated \(90^\circ\) clockwise.

Solution:

  • To draw the rotation, it will be helpful first if we find the coordinates of the vertices for the rotation.
  • To find the coordinates of the \(90^\circ\) clockwise rotation, we will apply the rule \((x,y)\rightarrow (y,-x)\).
  • So, we begin finding the coordinates of the vertices of the rotated quadrilateral.
    • \(A(-8,1) \rightarrow (1,8)\)
    • \(B(0,5) \rightarrow (5,0)\)
    • \(C(-2,9) \rightarrow (9,2)\)
    • \(D(-10,7) \rightarrow (7,10)\)
  • Now that we have the coordinates of the vertices for the reflected quadrilaterals, we can graph those and connect them with a polygon.

The quadrilateral EFGH represents the \(90^\circ\) rotation clockwise about the origin of ABCD.



Conclusion

Today, we explored how geometric transformations like reflections and rotations can be represented algebraically in the coordinate plane. You learned the rules for reflections across the x-axis, y-axis, \(y=x\), and \(y=-x\), as well as rotations of 90°, 180°, and 270°. Using these rules, you can now describe, represent, and graph both reflections and rotations. Understanding these algebraic representations helps you see how transformations work systematically and prepares you to tackle more complex sequences of transformations in the future!
