📚 VOCABULARY

3-Dimensional Figure: A solid shape that has length, width, and height, taking up space in three dimensions.

Apex: The highest point of a cone or pyramid, where the lateral faces or curved surface meet.

Area: The measure of space enclosed within a two-dimensional shape, expressed in square units.

Base: The face of a 3D figure that serves as a foundation and is used to determine surface area and volume.

Cone: A 3D shape with a circular base and a curved surface that tapers to a single point called the apex.

Cylinder: A 3D shape with two parallel circular bases connected by a curved surface.

Face: A flat surface of a 3D figure, such as the bases and lateral faces of prisms and pyramids.

Height: The perpendicular distance from the base to the top of a 3D figure.

Lateral Area: The sum of the areas of only the lateral faces (not including the base) of a 3D shape.

Lateral Face: A face of a 3D figure that is not a base, typically found in prisms and pyramids.

Net: A two-dimensional pattern that can be folded to form a 3D shape.

Perimeter: The total length of the boundary of a 2D shape, used in some surface area calculations.

Prism: A 3D figure with two parallel, congruent bases and rectangular lateral faces.

Pyramid: A 3D figure with a polygonal base and triangular lateral faces that meet at an apex.

Radius: A line segment from the center of a circle to any point on the circle’s circumference, used in circular base calculations.

Slant Height: The distance from the apex of a pyramid or cone to the edge of the base, measured along the lateral surface.

Sphere: A perfectly round 3D shape where all points are equidistant from the center.

Surface Area: The total area of all faces or curved surfaces of a 3D figure.

Total Surface Area: The sum of the lateral area and the area of all bases in a 3D figure.

Vertex: A point where edges or faces of a 3D figure meet.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore the concept of surface area and how to calculate it for various 3D figures, including cylinders, cones, and spheres. Surface area represents the total area of all the faces or curved surfaces of a shape. By understanding how to break these solids into their individual faces or curved surfaces, we can connect the idea of nets to their respective formulas. This skill is useful in many real-world applications, such as construction, packaging, and manufacturing. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to identify and apply the correct formula for surface area, analyze 3D solids, and solve real-world problems involving surface area.


Understanding Surface Area Conceptually

Surface area is the total area that covers a 3D shape. If we imagine unfolding a solid into a flat representation, we create a net, which helps visualize and calculate the total surface area.
Each type of solid has a specific set of faces or curved surfaces, and we use different formulas to calculate their total surface area.

Here is an example of a Right Cylinder and its net.

The net helps you to see the 2D shapes that fold to make the 3D solid. The surface area is just the area of all the surfaces or the 2D shapes.

In the cylinder, there is a rectangle for the lateral face and 2 circles for the top and bottom.

The net of a cone and sphere are not as functional to observe. The lateral face of a cone is a triangle with a curved base so that it can wrap around the circular base. This is not a 2D shape we are accustomed to viewing. A sphere is a curvature of 4 different circular nets.


Surface Area Formulas for Cylinders, Cones, and Spheres

Cylinder Cone Sphere

\(\Large SA=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh\)

Where:

  • \(r\) is the radius of the circular base
  • \(h\) is the height of the cylinder

Explanation: This includes two circular bases (\(2\pi r^2\)) and a rectangular lateral surface when unrolled (\(2\pi rh\)).

  • The bases of a cylinder are always circles and the lateral face is always a rectangle.
  • The length of the lateral rectangle is equal to the circumference of the circular base it wraps around.

\(\Large SA=\pi r^2+\pi rl\)

Where:

  • \(r\) is the radius of the circular base
  • \(l\) is the slant height of the lateral face

Explanation: The surface area consists of the circular base (\(\pi r^2\)) and the lateral curved surface (\(\pi rl\)).

  • The base of a cone is always a circle.
  • The lateral face is like a section of a circle, similar to the area of a sector.

\(\Large SA=4\pi r^2\)

Where

  • \(r\) is the radius of the sphere

Explanation: A sphere has only one continuous curved surface, so its surface area is four times the area of a circle.



Example 1

Find the surface area of the right cylinder shown. Round your answer to the nearest thousandths.

Solution:

  • To find the surface area of this cylinder, it will be easiest to use the formula.
  • We can think about it conceptually as 2 circles and a rectangle. The rectangle gets a little tricky as the width of the rectangle is the circumference of the circle. This is represented in the formula.
  • The formula \(SA=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh\) has the bits needed to think about conceptually but makes it easier to follow.
    • The \(2\pi r^2\) represents the area of the two bases.
    • The \(2\pi rh\) represents the area of the rectangle.
  • Since we know the radius, r, is 3 and the height, h, is 5, we can substitute the values into the formula and start simplifying. We will get an EXACT answer first, then we will use a calculator to approximate it.
  • Surface Area is \(SA=2\pi (3^2) + 2\pi (3)(5) \implies SA=2\pi (9) + 2\pi (15) \implies SA=18\pi +30\pi \implies SA=48\pi \; units^2\)
  • Approximate Surface Area is \(SA\approx 150.796\; units^2\)



Example 2

Find the surface area of the cone shown. Round your answer to the nearest hundredths.

Solution:

  • To find the surface area of this cone, it will be easiest to use the formula.
  • The formula \(SA=\pi r^2+\pi rl\) has the bits needed to think about conceptually but makes it easier to follow.
    • The \(\pi r^2\) represents the area of the base.
    • The \(\pi rl\) represents the area of the curved triangle that wraps around to create the lateral face.
  • Since we know the radius, r, is 3 and the slant height, l, is \(2\sqrt{13}\), we can substitute the values into the formula and start simplifying. We will get an EXACT answer first, then we will use a calculator to approximate it.
  • Surface Area is \(SA=\pi (4^2) +\pi (4)(2\sqrt{13}) \implies SA=16\pi + 8\pi \sqrt{13} \; units^2\)
  • Approximate Surface Area is \(SA\approx 140.88\; units^2\)



Example 3

Find the surface area of the sphere shown. Round to the nearest tenths.

Solution:

  • To find the surface area of this sphere, it will be easiest to use the formula.
  • The formula \(SA=4\pi r^2\) has the bits needed to think about conceptually but makes it easier to follow.
    • A sphere is the curvature of essentially 4 circles wrapped together.
  • Since we know the radius, r, is 4, we can substitute the values into the formula and start simplifying. We will get an EXACT answer first, then we will use a calculator to approximate it.
  • Surface Area is \(SA=4\pi (4^2) \implies SA=4\pi (16) \implies SA=64\pi \; units^2\)
  • Approximate Surface Area is \(SA\approx 201.1\; units^2\)



Conclusion

Understanding surface area is essential in geometry and real-world applications such as construction, product packaging, and engineering. By breaking down 3D figures into their nets, we can visualize and calculate their total surface area using formulas. Whether determining the amount of material needed for a water tank, a gift box, or a balloon covering, applying the correct surface area formula ensures accurate and practical results. With these skills, you can confidently tackle real-world problems involving cylinders, cones, and spheres.
