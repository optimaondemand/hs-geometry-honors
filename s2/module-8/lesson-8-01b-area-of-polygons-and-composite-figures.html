📚 VOCABULARY

Altitude: A perpendicular segment from a vertex of a triangle to the opposite side or the line containing the opposite side.

Apothem: A line segment from the center of a regular polygon perpendicular to one of its sides.

Area: The measure of the space enclosed within a two-dimensional shape, expressed in square units.

Base: The side of a geometric figure used as a reference for measurement, often paired with height in area calculations.

Center: The point in a circle or regular polygon that is equidistant from all points on the figure.

Circle: A set of all points in a plane that are equidistant from a given point called the center.

Composite Figure: A shape made up of two or more basic geometric figures.

Diameter: A straight line passing through the center of a circle and touching both sides of the circumference, equal to twice the radius.

Height: The perpendicular distance from the base to the highest point of a shape.

Heron’s Formula: A formula used to find the area of a triangle when the lengths of all three sides are known: \(A=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\) where \(s\) is the semi-perimeter and \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are the side lengths of the triangle.

Hexagon: A polygon with six sides and six angles.

Quadrilateral: A four-sided polygon with four angles.

Octagon: A polygon with eight sides and eight angles.

Parallelogram: A quadrilateral with opposite sides that are parallel and equal in length.

Pentagon: A polygon with five sides and five angles.

Perimeter: The total distance around the outside of a polygon.

Pi (π): A mathematical constant approximately equal to 3.14159, representing the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter.

Polygon: A closed two-dimensional shape formed by three or more straight sides.

Radius: A line segment from the center of a circle to any point on the circle’s circumference.

Rectangle: A quadrilateral with four right angles and opposite sides that are equal in length.

Rhombus: A parallelogram with all four sides of equal length.

Semi-Perimeter: Half of a polygon’s perimeter, often used in Heron’s formula.

Square: A quadrilateral with four equal sides and four right angles.

Trapezoid: A quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides.

Triangle: A polygon with three sides and three angles.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how to find the area of triangles when the height is not directly given, the area of polygons, and the area of composite figures that combine multiple 2D shapes. Sometimes, a figure’s dimensions are not explicitly provided, requiring us to use alternative methods such as Heron’s formula or trigonometric ratios. Additionally, understanding how to find the area of complex shapes by decomposing them into simpler figures is essential for solving both mathematical and real-world problems. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to apply different formulas to calculate areas, solve real-world application problems, and use properties of geometric figures to determine missing measurements.


Finding the Area of a Triangle Without a Given Height

When the height of a triangle is not provided, there are two common methods to find its area.


Method 1 - If all sides of the triangle are given

Heron's Formula - \(A=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\)

  • where \(s\) is the semi-perimeter - \(s=\frac{a+b+c}{2}\)


Method 2 - If 2 sides and the INCLUDED angle are given

Trigonometric Formula - \(A=\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C\)

  • where \(a\) and \(b\) are two sides of the triangle, and \(C\) is the included angle


Example 1

Find the area of \(\bigtriangleup ABC\) with side lengths 8, 9, and 12.

Solution:

  • To find the area of the triangle, we will use Heron's Formula since we are not given the altitude of the triangle.
  • We will use the three side lengths 8, 9, and 12 to first find the semi-perimeter.
  • Semi-perimeter - \(s=\frac{8+9+12}{2} \implies s=\frac{29}{2} \implies s=14.5\)
  • Now, we will use the semi-perimeter and the side lengths to substitute into Heron's Formula.
  • \(A=\sqrt{14.5(14.5-8)(14.5-9)(14.5-12)} \implies A=\sqrt{14.5(6.5)(5.5)(2.5)} \implies A=\sqrt{1295.9375} \implies A\approx 35.999\)
  • We find that the exact area of \(\bigtriangleup ABC\) is \(A=\sqrt{1295.9375}\) and the approximate area is \(A\approx 35.999\).


Example 2

Find the area of \(\bigtriangleup DEF\) where \(DE=8\), \(FE=9\), and \(m\angle E=37^\circ\).

Solution:

  • To find the area of \(\bigtriangleup DEF\), we will use the trigonometric formula.
  • We are given side lengths \(\overline{DE}\) and \(\overline{FE}\) with the included angle \(\angle E\).
  • We will substitute these values into the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C\).
  • \(A=\frac{1}{2}(8)(9)\sin (37) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(72)\sin (37) \implies A=36\sin (37) \implies A\approx 21.665\)
  • We find that the area of \(\bigtriangleup DEF\) is exactly \(A=36\sin (37)\) and approximately \(A\approx 21.665\).




Finding the Area of Polygons

Polygons are multi-sided shapes such as pentagons, hexagons, and octagons.

The area of a regular polygon (one where all sides and angles are equal) can be found using the formula:

\(A=\frac{1}{2}aP\)

  • where \(a\) is the apothem and \(P\) is the perimeter


Example 3

Find the area of the regular polygon.

Solution:

  • To find the area, we will use the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}aP\).
  • We are given the side length, 5, and the apothem, 3.4.
  • Since the polygon is regular, all the side lengths are equal.
  • First, we will find the perimeter of the polygon.
  • \(P=5\cdot 5 \implies P=25\)
  • Next, we will use the perimeter, P, and the apothem, a, in the formula.
  • \(A=\frac{1}{2}(3.4)(25) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(85) \implies A=42.5\)
  • We find the area of the regular pentagon is \(A=42.5\).



Example 4

Find the area of the regular polygon.

Solution:

  • To find the area, we will use the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}aP\).
  • We are given the side length, 8, and the apothem, 6.9.
  • Since the polygon is regular, all the side lengths are equal.
  • First, we will find the perimeter of the polygon.
  • \(P=6\cdot 8 \implies P=48\)
  • Next, we will use the perimeter, P, and the apothem, a, in the formula.
  • \(A=\frac{1}{2}(6.9)(48) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(331.2) \implies A=165.6\)
  • We find the area of the regular hexagon is \(A=165.6\).



Example 5

Find the length of the apothem in the hexagon shown with an area of \(42\;units^2\).

Solution:

  • To find the length of the apothem, we will substitute the values we know into the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}aP\).
  • We know the perimeter, P, is \(P=6\cdot 4 \implies P=24\).
  • We also the area, A, is 42.
  • \(42=\frac{1}{2}a(24) \implies 42=12a \implies \frac{42}{12}=\frac{12a}{12} \implies a=3.5\)
  • We find the length of the apothem is 3.5 units.



Example 6

Find the side length of the regular octagon shown with an area of \(43.2\;units^2\).

Solution:

  • To find the side length, we will substitute the values we know into the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}aP\).
  • We know the apothem, a, is 3.6.
  • We also the area, A, is 43.2.
  • We know that \(P=8s\) where \(s\) is the side length.
  • \(43.2=\frac{1}{2}(3.6)(8s) \implies 43.2=1.8(8a) \implies 43.2=14.4s \implies \frac{43.2}{14.4}=\frac{14.4s}{14.4} \implies s=3\)
  • We find the side length of the regular octagon is 3 units.




Finding the Area of Composite Figures

A composite figure consists of multiple simple shapes combined together. To find its total area:

  1. Decompose the figure into known shapes (triangles, rectangles, circles, etc.).
  2. Find the area of each individual shape using the appropriate formula.
  3. Add or subtract the areas as needed (subtract if a smaller shape is cut out from a larger one).



Example 7

Find the area of the composite figure.

Solution:

  • To find the area of the composite shape, we need to first identify what shapes we have that we need to find the area of.
  • On the bottom, we have a trapezoid.
  • On the right side, we have a triangle.
  • On the top, we have a semicircle.
  • We can begin with finding the area of the trapezoid using the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}(b_1+b_2)h\).
    • We know that \(b_1=11\) and \(h=4\).
    • We also know since the radius of the semicircle is 4, then the diameter is 8. The diameter also represents the top base of the trapezoid.
    • So, \(b_2=8\)
  • Area of Trapezoid - \(A=\frac{1}{2}(11+8)(4) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(19)(4) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(76) \implies A=38\)
  • Next, we will find the area of the triangle on the right.
    • We know that the base \(b=7.8\) and the height \(h=2.6\).
    • We will use the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}bh\)
  • Area of Triangle - \(A=\frac{1}{2}(7.8)(2.6) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(20.28) \implies A=10.14\)
  • Last, we can find the area of the semicircle which is half the area of a circle - \(A=\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2\).
    • We know that the radius \(r=4\).
  • Area of Semicircle - \(A=\frac{1}{2}(4^2)\pi \implies A=\frac{1}{2}16\pi \implies A=8\pi \implies A\approx 25.1327\)
  • To find the total area, we add all the areas of the three shapes we identified.
  • Total Area - \(A=38+10.14+8\pi \implies A=48.14+8\pi\) is the exact area.
  • Total Approximate Area - \(A\approx 38+10.14+25.1327 \implies A\approx 73.2727\).



Example 8

Find the area of the composite figure.

Solution:

  • To find the area of the composite shape, we need to first identify what shapes we have that we need to find the area of.
  • On the bottom, we have a trapezoid.
  • On the right side, we have an equilateral triangle.
  • On the top, we have a rectangle.
  • We can begin with finding the area of the trapezoid using the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}(b_1+b_2)h\).
    • We know that \(b_1=4\) and \(h=3.5\).
    • We also know the top length of the rectangle is 8, which is also the length of the bottom. The bottom length of the rectangle is also the top base of the trapezoid.
    • So, \(b_2=8\)
  • Area of Trapezoid - \(A=\frac{1}{2}(4+8)(3.5) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(12)(3.5) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(42) \implies A=21\)
  • Next, we will find the area of the equilateral triangle on the right.
    • We will use the formula for area of an equilateral triangle - \(A=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2\)
    • We know that the side length \(s=5.5\).
  • Area of Triangle - \(A=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(5.5)^2 \implies A=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(30.25) \implies A=7.5625\sqrt{3} \implies A\approx 13.0986\)
  • Last, we can find the area of the rectangle - \(A=lw\).
    • We know that the length \(l=8\).
    • We also know that the width is the same as the side length of the equailteral triangle.
    • So, the width \(w=5.5\).
  • Area of Rectangle - \(A=(8)(5.5) \implies A=44\)
  • To find the total area, we add all the areas of the three shapes we identified.
  • Total Area - \(A=21+44+7.5625\sqrt{3} \implies A=65+7.5625\sqrt{3}\) is the exact area.
  • Total Approximate Area - \(A\approx 21+44+13.0986 \implies A\approx 78.0986\).




Understanding these area concepts is crucial in practical applications such as:

  • Architecture and engineering – Designing buildings, bridges, and floor plans that incorporate complex shapes.
  • Landscaping and urban planning – Calculating areas of parks, roads, and plots of land.
  • Manufacturing and materials science – Estimating materials needed for creating objects with various geometric components.



Conclusion

Finding the area of geometric figures, whether simple or complex, is an essential skill in both mathematics and everyday life. When height is not provided in a triangle, we can use Heron’s formula or trigonometric methods. For polygons, we can apply the apothem-based formula for regular shapes or decompose irregular shapes into known figures. Composite figures require breaking them into simpler parts, calculating each area separately, and then summing or subtracting as necessary. These techniques not only help in solving textbook problems but also allow us to understand real-world applications in architecture, engineering, and design. With these skills, you can confidently analyze and solve a variety of area-related challenges.
