📚 VOCABULARY

3-Dimensional Figure: A solid shape that has length, width, and height, taking up space in three dimensions.

Apex: The highest point of a cone or pyramid, where the lateral faces or curved surface meet.

Base: The bottom or top surface of a 3D figure, often used as a reference for volume calculations.

Cone: A 3D shape with a circular base and a curved surface that tapers to a single point called the apex.

Cylinder: A 3D shape with two parallel circular bases connected by a curved surface.

Height: The perpendicular distance from the base to the top of a 3D figure.

Lateral Face: A face of a 3D figure that is not a base, typically found in prisms and pyramids.

Perimeter: The total length of the boundary of a 2D shape, used in some volume calculations when determining base area.

Pi (): A mathematical constant approximately equal to 3.14159, representing the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter.

Radius: A line segment from the center of a circle to any point on the circle’s circumference, used in circular base calculations.

Right Cylinder: A cylinder whose sides are perpendicular to its circular bases.

Right Prism: A prism whose lateral faces are perpendicular to its bases.

Slant Height: The distance from the apex of a pyramid or cone to the edge of the base, measured along the lateral surface.

Sphere: A perfectly round 3D shape where all points are equidistant from the center.

Vertex: A point where edges or faces of a 3D figure meet.

Volume: The amount of space a 3D shape occupies, measured in cubic units.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore the concept of volume, which measures the amount of space a 3D figure occupies. Understanding volume is essential in geometry and many real-world applications, including architecture, engineering, and population density studies.
To develop a strong conceptual understanding, we will connect volume to the base area multiplied by height and explore how the volume of pyramids and cones is one-third that of a prism or cylinder with the same base and height. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to identify and apply the correct formula for volume, analyze various 3D solids, and solve real-world problems involving volume.


Understanding Volume Conceptually

Volume measures how much space a 3D shape occupies, expressed in cubic units (e.g., cm³, m³).

For prisms and cylinders, volume is found by multiplying the base area by the height: \(V=Bh\).

For pyramids and cones, their volume is one-third of the volume of a prism or cylinder with the same base and height: \(V=\frac{1}{3}Bh\).

For spheres, the formula is different because a sphere is a perfectly round shape with no edges or vertices: \(V=\frac{3}{4}\pi r^3\).

This means that pyramids and cones take up only one-third of the space of a prism or cylinder with the same dimensions, which can be demonstrated using water-filling experiments or visualization techniques.

Example 1

Find the volume of the right rectangular prism.

Solution:

  • To find the volume of the right rectangular prism, we think of volume as the base stacked 5 high where each has a height of 1 unit.
  • So, if we find the area of the base, and then multiply it by 5, we will have the total volume. This is the formula \(V=Bh\).
  • We first begin by finding the area of the base. Since the base is a rectangle, we use the formula \(A=l\cdot w\).
    • Area of Base \(B=4\cdot 8 \implies B=32\)
  • To find the volume, we multiply that by the height.
    • \(V=32\cdot 5 \implies V=160\)
  • The volume is \(160\; cubic\; units\).



Example 2

Find the volume of the right rectangular pyramid.

Solution:

  • To find the volume of the right rectangular pyramid, we think of volume as the base stacked 5 high where each has a height of 1 unit.
  • The biggest difference is we need to take \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the total volume to find the volume of the pyramid.
  • So, if we find the area of the base, multiply it by 5 and then take a third of that, we will have the volume of the pyramid. This is the formula \(V=\frac{1}{3}Bh\).
  • We first begin by finding the area of the base. Since the base is a rectangle, we use the formula \(A=l\cdot w\).
    • Area of Base \(B=4\cdot 8 \implies B=32\)
  • To find the volume, we multiply that by the height and take a third.
    • \(V=\frac{1}{3}(32\cdot 5) \implies V=\frac{1}{3}(160) \implies V=\frac{160}{3}\)
    • We leave it in fraction form since dividing by 3 creates a repeating decimal and thus would create an approximate answer and not the exact volume.
  • The volume is \(\frac{160}{3}\; cubic\; units\).



Example 3

Find the volume of the right pentagonal prism.

Solution:

  • To find the volume of the right pentagonal prism, we think of volume as the base stacked 7 high where each base has a height of 1 unit.
  • So, if we find the area of the base, and then multiply it by 7, we will have the total volume. This is the formula \(V=Bh\).
  • We first begin by finding the area of the base. Since the base is a pentagon, we use the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}aP\).
    • We need to first find the perimeter of the base - \(P=5\cdot 3 \implies P=15\).
    • The length of the apothem, \(a\), is 2.06 as shown.
    • Area of Base \(B=\frac{1}{2}(2.06)(15) \implies B=15.45\)
  • To find the volume, we multiply that by the height.
    • \(V=15.45\cdot 7 \implies V=108.15\)
  • The volume is \(108.15\; cubic\; units\).



Example 4

Find the volume of the right hexagonal pyramid. Round to the nearest hundredths.

Solution:

  • To find the volume of the right hexagonal pyramid, we think of volume as the base stacked 6 high where each has a height of 1 unit.
  • Then we need to take \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the total volume to find the volume of the pyramid.
  • So, if we find the area of the base, multiply it by 6 and then take a third of that, we will have the volume of the pyramid. This is the formula \(V=\frac{1}{3}Bh\).
  • We first begin by finding the area of the base. Since the base is a hexagon, we use the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}aP\).
    • We need to first find the perimeter of the base - \(P=6\cdot 4 \implies P=24\).
    • The length of the apothem, \(a\), is 3.46.
    • Area of Base \(B=\frac{1}{2}(3.46)(24) \implies B=41.52\)
  • To find the volume, we multiply that by the height and take a third.
    • \(V=\frac{1}{3}(41.52\cdot 6) \implies V=\frac{1}{3}(249.12) \implies V=\frac{249.12}{3}\)
    • Since the volume is a decimal, we will take a third of that and write the volume as a decimal answer.
  • The volume is \(83.04\; cubic\; units\).

Example 5

Find the volume of the right cylinder.

Solution:

  • To find the volume of the cylinder, we think of volume as the base stacked 3 high where each base has a height of 1 unit.
  • So, if we find the area of the base, and then multiply it by 3, we will have the total volume. This is the formula \(V=Bh\).
  • Since the base is a circle and the area of a circle is \(A=\pi r^2\), then we get the volume formula \(V=\pi r^2h\)
  • We first begin by finding the area of the base. Since the base is a circle, we use the formula \(A=\pi r^2\).
    • Area of Base \(B=\pi (5)^2 \implies B=25\pi \)
  • To find the volume, we multiply that by the height.
    • \(V=25\pi \cdot 3 \implies V=75\pi \)
    • This is the exact volume.
    • If asked for the approximate volume, plug that into a calculator using the pi key.
  • The EXACT volume is \(75\pi \; cubic\; units\).
  • The approximate volume is \(V\approx 235.62\; cubic\; units\).



Example 6

Find the volume of the right cone.

Solution:

  • To find the volume of the cone, we think of volume as the base stacked 3 high where each has a height of 1 unit for the cylinder.
  • Then we take \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the total volume to find the volume of the cone.
  • So, if we find the area of the base, multiply it by 3 and then take a third of that, we will have the volume of the pyramid. This is the formula \(V=\frac{1}{3}Bh\).
  • The base is a circle and the area of a circle is \(A=\pi r^2\). This substituted in for \(B\) creates the formula \(V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h\).
  • We first begin by finding the area of the base. Since the base is a circle, we use the formula \(A=\pi r^2\).
    • Area of Base \(B=\pi (5)^2 \implies B=25\pi \)
  • To find the volume, we multiply that by the height and take a third.
    • \(V=\frac{1}{3}(25\pi \cdot 3) \implies V=\frac{1}{3}(75\pi) \implies V=\frac{75\pi}{3} \implies V=25\pi \)
    • This is the exact volume. If the question asks for the approximate volume, then plug that into a calculator and use the pi key.
  • The EXACT volume is \(25\pi \; cubic\; units\).
  • The approximate volume is \(V\approx 78.54\; cubic\; units\).



Example 7

Find the volume of the sphere.

Solution:

  • To find the volume of the sphere, we will use the volume formula \(V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\) where r is the radius of the sphere.
  • To find the volume, we substitute in the radius which is 5.
      • \(V=\frac{4}{3}(\pi (5)^3 \implies V=\frac{4}{3}(125\pi) \implies V=\frac{500\pi}{3}\)
      • This is the exact volume. If the question asks for the approximate volume, then plug that into a calculator and use the pi key.
  • The EXACT volume is \(\frac{500\pi}{3} \; cubic\; units\).
  • The approximate volume is \(V\approx 523.599\; cubic\; units\).



Applying Volume in Real-World Problems

Example 1: Water Storage in a Cylindrical Tank

A cylindrical water tank has a radius of 5 meters and a height of 10 meters.

How much water can the tank hold? Round to the nearest hundredth of a meter.

Solution:

  • Since the volume we are looking for is of a cylindrical tank, we will use the volume formula for a cylinder - \(V=\pi r^2h\).
  • The radius of the tank is 5 meters and the height is 10 meters.
  • \(V=\pi (5)^2 \cdot 10 \implies V=\pi (25)(10) \implies V=250\pi \)
  • We will plug this into our calculator at once to get the approximate volume.
  • The volume of the tank is \(785.40\; m^3\).



Example 2: Population Density in a City

A city has a population of 500,000 people and occupies a roughly rectangular area measuring 20 miles by 15 miles with an average building height of 50 feet.

What is the population density per cubic mile?

Solution:

  • First, find the volume of the occupied space.
  • Since the base of the city is a rectangle, use the volume formula for a rectangular prism \(V=Bh\).
    • Area of the base is \(B=l\cdot w \implies B=20\cdot 15 \implies B=300\)
  • Next, we need to convert the height into miles so that all values are in the same unit.
    • 1 mile = 5,280 feet
    • \(h=\frac{50}{5280} \implies h\approx 0.009469697\; miles\)
  • Now we can calculate the volume.
    • \(V=300(0.009469697) V\approx 2.8409\; cubic\; miles\)
  • Finally, to calculate the population density, we divide the total population by the total volume.
  • \(\frac{500000}{2.8409} \approx 176,000\)
  • This means there is approximately 176,000 people per cubic mile.



Conclusion

Understanding volume is essential for solving real-world problems in engineering, urban planning, storage, and construction. The formula for volume follows a consistent base area times height pattern, but pyramids and cones only occupy one-third of their prism or cylinder counterparts.
By applying these volume formulas, we can determine capacity, efficiency, and density in practical scenarios. Whether calculating water storage, city population density, or tent space, mastering volume ensures accurate and useful problem-solving skills in both mathematics and everyday life.
