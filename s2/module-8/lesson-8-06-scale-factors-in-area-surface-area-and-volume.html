Scale Factors in Area, Surface Area, and Volume
Lesson
📚 VOCABULARY

3-Dimensional Figure: A solid shape that has length, width, and height, and occupies space in three dimensions.

Area: The amount of surface enclosed within a 2D shape, measured in square units.

Base Area: The area of the base face of a 3D figure, used in volume and surface area calculations.

Cone: A 3D shape with a circular base and a curved surface that tapers to a single point called the apex.

Cylinder: A 3D figure with two parallel, congruent circular bases connected by a curved surface.

Dilation: A transformation that enlarges or reduces a figure by a scale factor relative to a center point.

Enlargement: A dilation where the scale factor is greater than 1, resulting in a larger figure.

Lateral Area: The area of all the sides or curved surfaces of a 3D figure, excluding the bases.

Prism: A 3D figure with two parallel, congruent bases and rectangular lateral faces.

Pyramid: A 3D figure with a polygonal base and triangular faces that meet at a single vertex (apex).

Radius: The distance from the center of a circle or sphere to any point on its boundary.

Reduction: A dilation where the scale factor is between 0 and 1, resulting in a smaller figure.

Scale Factor: The ratio of a length in the image to the corresponding length in the original figure during a dilation.

Similar Figures: Figures that have the same shape but different sizes; their corresponding angles are equal, and corresponding sides are proportional.

Sphere: A perfectly round 3D shape where every point on the surface is the same distance from the center.

Surface Area: The total area of all the faces or curved surfaces of a 3D figure.

Volume: The amount of space occupied by a 3D figure, measured in cubic units.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how dilations affect 2D and 3D shapes, and specifically how they impact area, surface area, and volume. Dilations change the size of a shape but preserve its proportions. We’ll learn how a scale factor affects each of these measurements and apply our understanding to mathematical problems and real-world applications. By the end of the lesson, you will be able to calculate the new area, surface area, or volume of a dilated figure using the scale factor, and apply this knowledge to problems involving objects in construction, packaging, and design.


How Scale Factor Affects Area, Surface Area, and Volume

  • If a 2D shape is dilated by a scale factor \(k\):
    \(New\; Area=k^2\cdot Original\; Area\)

  • If a 3D shape is dilated by a scale factor \(k\):
    \(New\; Surface\; Area=k^2 \cdot Original\; Surface\; Area\)
    \(New\; Volume=k^3 \cdot Original\; Volume\)



Example 1

A square has an area of \(25\: cm^2\). It is dilated by a scale factor of 3.

What is the area of the enlarged square?

Solution:

  • This dilation is an enlargement since the scale factor is greater than one. The square grew by a factor of 3, or it is 3 times larger.
  • If the scale factor for the dilation is 3, then the scale factor for the area will be \(3^2\) or 9.
  • To find the area of the enlarged square, we multiply the area of the original square by the area scale factor of 9.
  • Area of New Square = \(9\cdot 25\) = \(225\; cm^2\)


Example 2

A rectangular prism has a surface area of \(120\; in^2\). It is dilated by a scale factor of 0.5.

What is the surface area of the reduced rectangular prism?

Solution:

  • This dilation is a reduction since the scale factor is less than one. The prism is shrinking by one-half.
  • The scale factor of the side lengths is \(k=0.5\). Therefore, the scale factor of the surface area is \(k^2\) or \(0.5^2=0.25\).
  • Notice that even though the side lengths are shrinking by one-half, the overall surface area only shrinks by a quarter.
  • To find the new surface area of the reduced prism, we multiply the original surface area by the surface area scale factor.
  • Surface Area of Reduced Rectangular Prism = \(0.25\cdot 120\) = \(30\; in^2\).


Example 3

A cone has a surface area of \(200\; m^2\). It undergoes a dilation with a scale factor of \(\frac{4}{3}\).

What is the surface area of the enlarged cone?

Solution:

  • This dilation is an enlargement since the scale factor is greater than one.
  • The scale factor is \(k=\frac{4}{3}\). So, the scale factor for the surface area is \(k^2\) or \((\frac{4}{3})^2=\frac{16}{9}\).
  • To find the new surface area, we multiply the original surface area by the surface area scale factor \(k=\frac{16}{9}\).
  • Surface Area of Enlarged Cone = \(200\cdot \frac{16}{9}\) = \(\frac{3200}{9}\) or \(355\frac{5}{9}\; m^2\).


Example 4

A cylinder has a volume of \(1500\; cm^3\). It undergoes a dilation with a scale factor of \(\frac{3}{2}\).

What is the volume of the enlarged cylinder?

Solution:

  • This dilation is an enlargement since the scale factor is greater than one.
  • The scale factor is \(k=\frac{3}{2}\). So, the scale factor for the volume is \(k^3\) or \((\frac{3}{2})^3=\frac{27}{8}\).
  • To find the new volume, we multiply the original volume by the volume scale factor \(k=\frac{27}{8}\).
  • Volume of Enlarged Cylinder = \(1500\cdot \frac{27}{8}\) = \(\frac{10125}{2}\) or \(5062.5\; cm^3\).



Real-World Applications of Scale Factor in 3D Figures

Example 1

An architect creates a scale model of a building with a scale factor of 1:20. The model has a volume of \(0.5\; m^3\).

What is the volume of the actual building?

Solution:

  • This dilation is an enlargement since the scale factor is greater than one.
  • The scale factor is \(k=20\). So, the scale factor for the volume is \(k^3\) or \((20^3=8000)\).
  • To find the actual building's volume, we multiply the model volume by the volume scale factor \(k=8000\).
  • Volume of Actual Building = \(8000\cdot 0.5\) = \(4000\; m^3\).


Example 2

A company scales down a product for packaging. The original container had a surface area of \(600\; cm^2\).

A smaller version is made using a scale factor of 0.6.

What is the surface area of the smaller packaging?

Solution:

  • This dilation is a reduction since the scale factor is less than one.
  • The scale factor is \(k=0.6\). So, the scale factor for the surface area is \(k^2\) or \((0.6^2=0.36)\).
  • To find the surface area of the smaller packaging, we multiply the original surface area by the surface area scale factor \(k=0.36\).
  • Surface Area of Smaller Packaging = \(600\cdot 0.36\) = \(216\; cm^2\).



Conclusion

Understanding how dilations affect area, surface area, and volume is critical for solving both mathematical and real-world problems. As we’ve learned, area and surface area are affected by the square of the scale factor, while volume is affected by the cube.
This relationship helps us solve problems in fields like engineering, architecture, biology, and design, where models and real objects must be compared, scaled up, or scaled down. Mastering this concept allows you to analyze how size changes impact space, material use, and functionality.
