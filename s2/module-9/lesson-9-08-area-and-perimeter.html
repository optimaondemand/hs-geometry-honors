📚 VOCABULARY

Area: The number of square units needed to cover the surface of a two-dimensional shape.

Base: The bottom edge or side of a geometric figure, often used as a reference for measuring height.

Coordinates: An ordered pair of numbers \((x,y)\) that locates a point on the coordinate plane.

Coordinate Plane: A two-dimensional surface formed by a horizontal number line (x-axis) and a vertical number line (y-axis) that intersect at the origin.

Diagonal: A line segment connecting two non-adjacent vertices of a polygon.

Distance Formula: A formula used to calculate the length between two points on the coordinate plane \(d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\).

Height: The perpendicular distance from the base to the top or opposite side of a shape.

Length: The measurement of how long a side or segment is, usually the longer dimension of a rectangle or parallelogram.

Midpoint: The point that is exactly halfway between two endpoints of a segment.

Origin: The point \((0,0)\) where the x-axis and y-axis intersect on the coordinate plane.

Perimeter: The total distance around a two-dimensional shape, found by adding the lengths of all sides.

Polygon: A closed figure made up of three or more straight-line segments connected end to end.

Quadrilateral: A polygon with exactly four sides and four angles.

Right Triangle: A triangle that has one angle measuring exactly 90 degrees.

Side Length: The measurement of one edge of a polygon or triangle.

Square Units: Units used to measure area, such as square centimeters or square feet.

Triangle: A polygon with three sides and three angles.

Vertex: A point where two sides of a polygon meet.

Width: The shorter dimension of a shape, typically measured perpendicular to the length or base.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how to find the perimeter and area of various two-dimensional shapes when they are drawn on the coordinate plane. You will learn how to calculate perimeter and area using formulas and coordinate geometry methods for triangles, rectangles, parallelograms, rhombuses, and even composite figures. These skills will help you solve both mathematical and real-world problems, such as determining the fencing needed around a garden or the space inside a playground. With the help of formulas and coordinate points, you can calculate these values even when the shapes are slanted or positioned diagonally.


Finding Perimeter on the Coordinate Plane

To find the perimeter of any polygon:

  1. Use the Distance Formula to calculate the length of each side between consecutive vertices.

  2. Add all the side lengths together.


Finding Area on the Coordinate Plane

To find the area of any polygon:

  1. Use the Distance Formula to calculate the length of each side between consecutive vertices.

  2. Use different area formulas depending on the shape.

    • Area of a Triangle: \(A=\frac{1}{2}bh\)
    • Area of a Rectangle: \(A=lw\)
    • Area of Parallelogram: \(A=bh\)
    • Rhombus: \(A=\frac{1}{2}d_1d_2\)
    • Trapezoid: \(A=\frac{1}{2}(b_1+b_2)h\)


Composite Figures

For shapes made of more than one polygon:

  1. Break the figure into simpler shapes like triangles, rectangles, or parallelograms.

  2. Find the area of each part.

  3. Add the areas together to get the total.

Use the Distance Formula to find missing side lengths or diagonal lengths if the shapes are not aligned with the axes.


Finding Perimeter

Example 1

Find the perimeter of the square shown on the coordinate plane.

Solution:

  • To find the perimeter, we need to find the side lengths using the distance formula.
  • Since a square has all equal sides, we can find one side and multiply it by 4.
  • Let's begin by identifying the coordinates of two vertices for one side of the square.
  • \(A(-5,2)\) and \(B(-1,6)\).
  • Now we can use the coordinates and the distance formula to find the length of \(\overline{AB}\).
  • \(d=\sqrt{(-5-(-1))^2+(2-6)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{(-4)^2+(-4)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{16+16} \implies d=\sqrt{32}\)
  • The length of AB is \(\sqrt{32}\).
  • We multiply that by 4 to find the perimeter.

The perimeter of the square is \(4\sqrt{32}\), which is approximately 22.63.



Example 2

Find the perimeter of the parallelogram shown on the coordinate plane.

Solution:

  • To find the perimeter of this parallelogram, we need to find the lengths of the sides.
  • We know that in a parallelogram, opposite sides are congruent.
  • So, we can find the length of \(\overline{GE}\) and the length of \(\overline{EN}\).
  • Then we can use the perimeter formula \(P=2l+2w\).
  • Let's begin by identifying the coordinates for the vertices for \(\overline{GE}\) and finding its length.
    • \(G(-5,-1)\) and \(E(-4,4)\)
    • \(d=\sqrt{(-5-(-4))^2+(-1-4)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{(-1)^2+(-5)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{1+25} \implies d=\sqrt{26}\).
    • The length of GE is \(\sqrt{26}\).
  • Next, we will identify the coordinates of the vertices for \(\overline{EN}\) and find its length.
    • \(E(-4,4)\) and \(N(2,8)\)
    • \(d=\sqrt{(-4-2)^2+(4-8)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{(-6)^2+(-4)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{36+16} \implies d=\sqrt{52}\)
    • The length of EN is \(\sqrt{52}\).
  • Now, we can find the perimeter. NOTE that I have not found an approximate length for the sides yet! Use them in their EXACT form until the very last step. Then plug everything into the calculcator at one time. Otherwise, your answer could be off and will be marked wrong.

The perimeter of the parallelogram is \(P=2\sqrt{26}+2\sqrt{52}\) or \(P\approx 24.62\).



Finding Area

Example 3

Find the area of the parallelogram shown on the coordinate plane.

Solution:

  • To find the area of the parallelogram, we will use the formula \(A=bh\) where b is the base and h is the height or altitude.
  • Remember that the height of a parallelogram is the perpendicular length from the base of the parallelogram. It is not a slant side.
  • So, we first, we need to find the length of the base \(\overline{GH}\).
    • \(G(-5,-1)\) and \(H(1,3)\)
    • \(d=\sqrt{(-5-1)^2+(-1-3)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{(-6)^2+(-4)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{36+16} \implies d=\sqrt{52}\).
  • Next, we need to find the length of the height \(\overline{EF}\).
    • \(E(-4,4)\) and \(F(-2,1)\)
    • \(d=\sqrt{(-4-(-2))^2+(4-(1))^2} \implies d=\sqrt{(-2)^2+(3)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{4+9} \implies d=\sqrt{13}\).
  • Now, we can find the area by multiplying the two lengths A=bh.
  • When you multiply square roots, you multiply the numbers inside the root.

The area of the parallelogram is \(A=\sqrt{52} \cdot \sqrt{13} \implies A=\sqrt{676} \implies A=26\).



Example 4

Find the area of the trapezoid shown on the coordinate plane.

Solution:

  • To find the area of the trapezoid, we will use the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}(b_1+b_2)h\) where \(b_1\) is the length of the bottom base, \(b_2\) is the length of the top base, and \(h\) is the perpendicular height.
  • We need to find the lengths of the bases and the height.
  • Since the bases and heights are not slanted, we can count the distances.
    • \(b_1=|(-6-5)| \implies b_1=|-11| \implies b_1=11\)
    • \(b_2=|(-3-3)| \implies b_2=|-6| \implies b_2=6\)
    • \(h=|(3-(-2))| \implies h=|5| \implies h=5\).
  • Now that we know the lengths of all the pieces, we can find the area of the trapezoid.

The area of the trapezoid is \(A=\frac{1}{2}(11+6)5 \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(17)(5) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(85) \implies A=42.5\).



Example 5 - Real-World

A farmer is designing a triangular pen on a coordinate grid with corners at \(A(1,2)\), \(B(5,2)\), and \(C(3,6)\).

He needs to fence in the pen and lay down some sod.

How much fencing will the farmer need and how much sod will he need?

Solution:

  • To find how much fencing the farmer will need, we need to find the perimeter of the shape.
  • To find how much sod the farmer will need, we need to find the area of the shape.
  • We will begin plotting the points and seeing what the shape is and how to find the lengths of the pieces needed.
  • To find the perimeter, we need to begin by finding the lengths of all the sides.
    • \(OP=|5-1| \implies OP=4\)
    • For \(\overline{OQ}\), we need the coordinates of O and Q - \(O(1,2)\) and \(Q(3,6)\).
  • \(d=\sqrt{(1-3)^2+(2-6)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{(-2)^2+(-4)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{4+16} \implies d=\sqrt{20}\)Now, we
    • For \(\overline{QP}\), we need the coordinates of Q and P - \(Q(3,6)\) and \(P(5,2)\).
    • \(d=\sqrt{(3-5)^2+(6-2)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{(-2)^2+(4)^2} \implies d=\sqrt{20}\)
  • Now, we can find the perimeter by adding all sides and finding the amount of fencing needed. We will round to the nearest whole unit. Remember to use the exact lengths in the formula, then plug into the calculator all at once. DO NOT calculate in pieces and round pieces to do more calculations.
  • \(P=4+\sqrt{20}+\sqrt{20} \implies P\approx 13\)
  • Now, we will find the area to determine how much sod he needs and again, we will round up to the nearest whole unit.
  • To find the area we will use the formula \(A=\frac{1}{2}bh\). We have the length of the base \(\overline{OP}\).
  • We need the length of the height which will be the perpendicular segment from the base. This will be a line connecting Q to the base.
  • Since it is vertical, we can find the length by subtracting the y-values.
    • \(h=|6-2| \implies h=4\).
  • Now we can find the area of the triangle.
  • \(A=\frac{1}{2}(4)(4) \implies A=\frac{1}{2}(16) \implies A=8\).

We find that the farmer will need 13 units of fencing and 8 units of sod to complete his pen.



Conclusion

By using coordinate geometry and appropriate formulas, you can confidently find the perimeter and area of many different shapes—even when they are not perfectly aligned. Whether you’re working with triangles, rectangles, rhombuses, parallelograms, or composite figures, tools like the Distance Formula and knowledge of base and height relationships allow you to solve real-world and mathematical problems with accuracy. With practice, finding perimeter and area on the coordinate plane will become a powerful strategy in your geometry toolkit.
