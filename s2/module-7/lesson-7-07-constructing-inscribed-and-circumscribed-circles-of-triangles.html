Constructing Inscribed and Circumscribed Circles of Triangles

📚 VOCABULARY

Angle Bisector: A line or segment that divides an angle into two equal parts.

Circumcenter: The point where the perpendicular bisectors of a triangle’s sides intersect; it is the center of the circumscribed circle.

Circumscribed Circle: A circle that passes through all three vertices of a triangle, with its center at the circumcenter.

Compass: A tool used to draw circles and arcs.

Concurrent Lines: Three or more lines that intersect at a single point.

Incenter: The point where the angle bisectors of a triangle intersect; it is the center of the inscribed circle.

Inscribed Circle: A circle that fits inside a triangle, touching all three sides at exactly one point each, with its center at the incenter.

Midpoint: The point that divides a segment into two equal parts.

Orthocenter: The point where the altitudes of a triangle intersect.

Perpendicular Bisector: A line that divides a segment into two equal parts at a 90-degree angle.

Radius: A segment from the center of a circle to any point on the circle.

Straightedge: A tool used to draw straight lines without measurement markings.

Triangle: A three-sided polygon with three angles that sum to 180°.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore the difference between a circumscribed circle and an inscribed circle in a triangle. A circumscribed circle is a circle that passes through all three vertices of a triangle, while an inscribed circle is a circle that touches all three sides of a triangle.

We will first define and understand the properties of each type of circle. Then, we will learn the step-by-step construction of a circumscribed circle using the perpendicular bisectors of a triangle. Finally, we will construct an inscribed circle using the angle bisectors of a triangle. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to construct both circles using a compass and straightedge and understand their geometric significance.



Understanding Circumscribed and Inscribed Circles

Circumscribed Circle

  • A circumscribed circle is a circle that passes through all three vertices of a triangle.
  • The center of the circumscribed circle is the circumcenter, which is the point where the perpendicular bisectors of the triangle’s three sides intersect.
  • The circumcenter is equidistant from all three vertices, meaning the radius of the circle is the same from the circumcenter to each vertex.

Inscribed Circle

  • An inscribed circle is a circle that fits inside a triangle and touches all three sides at exactly one point each.
  • The center of the inscribed circle is the incenter, which is the point where the angle bisectors of the triangle intersect.
  • The incenter is equidistant from all three sides, meaning the radius of the inscribed circle is the same distance from the incenter to each side.




Constructing a Circumscribed Circle

  1. Use the Given Triangle

    • Use the given triangle.

  2. Find the Perpendicular Bisectors

    • Construct the perpendicular bisector for each side.
      • Begin by placing the compass at one of the vertices and opening the compass to a width of a little more than half the side length.
      • Draw half an arc starting above and going below the segment.
      • Move the compass to the vertex representing the other end point of that same side.
      • Keeping the compass width the same, draw an arc starting above and going below the side. Be sure the two arcs intersect above and below that side.
    • Use a straightedge to draw a line through the intersection of the arcs, forming the perpendicular bisector.
    • Repeat these steps for the other two sides of the triangle. You will have drawn 3 perpendicular bisectors.

  3. Find the Circumcenter

    • The three perpendicular bisectors will intersect at a single point—this is the circumcenter.
    • The circumcenter is the center of the circumscribed circle. Mark this point.

  4. Draw the Circumscribed Circle

    • Place the compass at the circumcenter and adjust it so the pencil reaches one of the triangle’s vertices.
    • Draw a circle that passes through all three vertices.
    • This is the circumscribed circle around the triangle.

Constructing an Inscribed Circle

  1. Use the Given Triangle

    • Use the given triangle (scalene, isosceles, or equilateral).

  2. Find the Angle Bisectors

    • Use a compass to construct the angle bisector of each interior angle:
      • Place the compass at a vertex and draw an arc that crosses both sides of the angle.
      • Move the compass to where the arc crosses each side and draw two additional arcs that intersect inside the triangle.
      • Use a straightedge to draw a line from the vertex through the intersection of the arcs—this is the angle bisector.
    • Repeat these steps for the remaining two angles so that there are 3 angle bisectors drawn.

  3. Find the Incenter

    • The three angle bisectors will intersect at a single point—this is the incenter. Mark this point.
    • The incenter is the center of the inscribed circle.

  4. Draw the Inscribed Circle

    • From the incenter, draw a perpendicular line to any side of the triangle to find the radius of the circle.
      • Place the compass at the point representing the incenter.
      • Open the compass wide enough to intersect the segment in two places.
      • Draw an arc that crosses the segment at two points.
      • Place the compass at one of the points where that arc intersects the side. Open a little more than halfway between the two intersection points.
      • Draw an arc above and below the side.
      • Move the compass to the other intersection and repeat. Make sure the arcs above and below intersect each from.
      • Using the straightedge, connect the two intersections above and below the side. Mark the point on the triangle's side.
    • Place the compass at the incenter, set it to the length of the radius by opening it to the length of the point on the side of the triangle you just created.
    • Draw a circle inside the triangle that touches all three sides.
    • This is the inscribed circle within the triangle.

Conclusion

By following these steps, you can construct a perpendicular line accurately using only a compass and straightedge. This construction is fundamental in geometry, engineering, and technical drawing, as perpendicular lines help define right angles and symmetry in various geometric applications.
