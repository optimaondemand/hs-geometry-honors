📚 VOCABULARY

Base: The bottom surface of a solid figure that is typically used as a reference for measurement or slicing.

Circle: A round two-dimensional shape where all points are equidistant from the center.

Cone: A three-dimensional figure with a circular base that tapers smoothly to a single point called the apex.

Cross-section: The two-dimensional shape created when a solid figure is sliced by a plane.

Cylinder: A three-dimensional figure with two parallel circular bases connected by a curved surface.

Ellipse: A stretched circle, appearing as an oval; formed when a cone is sliced at an angle to its base.

Face: A flat surface that forms part of the boundary of a solid object.

Parallel: Lines or planes that are always the same distance apart and never meet.

Perpendicular: Lines or planes that intersect at a 90-degree angle.

Plane: A flat surface that extends infinitely in all directions.

Polygon: A closed two-dimensional figure made up of straight line segments.

Prism: A solid figure with two parallel, congruent polygonal bases connected by rectangular faces.

Pyramid: A solid figure with a polygonal base and triangular faces that meet at a common point (the apex).

Rectangle: A quadrilateral with four right angles and opposite sides that are equal in length.

Slice: A cut made through a solid figure, usually by a plane, to reveal a cross-section.

Solid: A three-dimensional geometric figure with length, width, and height.

Square: A quadrilateral with four equal sides and four right angles.

Triangle: A polygon with three sides and three angles.

Three-Dimensional: A shape or figure that has depth in addition to length and width.

Two-Dimensional: A flat shape or figure that has only length and width.

Vertical: Positioned upright or perpendicular to the horizon or base.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how slicing three-dimensional figures can create two-dimensional shapes called cross-sections. Understanding cross-sections helps us visualize and analyze the structure of solids in geometry. We will focus on slicing common 3D figures—such as prisms, cylinders, pyramids, and cones—using planes that are either parallel or perpendicular to the base. You'll learn how the orientation of the slicing plane affects the resulting two-dimensional shape and how to identify whether a slice is parallel or perpendicular to the base of a solid.


What Is a Cross-Section?

A cross-section is the flat, two-dimensional shape that results when a plane slices through a three-dimensional solid. Think of cutting through a watermelon—the shape you see on the cut surface is a cross-section. These slices can help us better understand the internal makeup of the solid.


Cross-Sections Parallel to the Base
When a solid is sliced with a plane that is parallel to its base, the resulting cross-section will typically be the same shape as the base.

  • For a right prism or right cylinder, slicing parallel to the base will produce a congruent shape to the base.

  • For a right pyramid or right cone, slicing parallel to the base produces a shape similar to the base, but smaller. 


Cross-Sections Perpendicular to the Base
When a solid is sliced with a plane that is perpendicular to the base, the shape of the cross-section depends on the orientation and the structure of the solid.

  • For a right prism or right cylinder, slicing perpendicular to the base through the center usually creates a rectangle or square (depending on the dimensions).

  • For a right pyramid or right cone, slicing perpendicular to the base can result in triangles.



Identifying and Predicting Cross-Sections

To identify the cross-section shape:

  1. Look at the shape of the base and determine if the slicing plane is parallel or perpendicular to it.

  2. If the slice is parallel, expect a shape identical or similar to the base.

  3. If the slice is perpendicular, consider how the slicing plane intersects the sides and apex of the figure to predict the resulting shape.



Example 1

A right cylinder is sliced parallel to its base.
What is the shape of the cross-section that is formed?

Answer:
The cross-section will be a circle, because slicing parallel to the base of a right cylinder results in a shape that is congruent to the circular base.



Example 2

A pentagonal pyramid is sliced, and the resulting cross-section is a pentagon.
Was the pyramid sliced parallel or perpendicular to its base?

Answer:

  • The pyramid was sliced parallel to its base.
  • Remember, a slice parallel to the base of a pentagonal pyramid results in a smaller, similar pentagon.
  • This is because a slice parallel to the base of any pyramid or cone will result in a shape similar to the base.



Conclusion

Understanding cross-sections helps students connect three-dimensional geometry to the flat, two-dimensional shapes they see every day. By recognizing the difference between parallel and perpendicular slices and predicting the resulting shapes, you can visualize solids more clearly and describe their internal structures. As you continue exploring geometry, keep practicing how slicing direction affects the cross-section—this foundational skill will be useful in both mathematical reasoning and real-world applications.
