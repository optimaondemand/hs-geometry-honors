Rotations of 2D Solids

📚 VOCABULARY

Axis of Rotation: A fixed line around which a two-dimensional figure is rotated to generate a three-dimensional solid; the axis may lie along an edge of the figure, inside the figure, or external to it.

Circle: The set of all points in a plane that are equidistant from a fixed center point; the distance from the center to any point on the circle is the radius.

Cone: A three-dimensional solid with a circular base that tapers to a single point called the apex; generated by rotating a right triangle around one of its legs.

Cross-Section: The two-dimensional shape exposed when a three-dimensional solid is cut by a plane; for a solid of revolution, the original 2D figure is often the cross-section of the resulting solid.

Cylinder: A three-dimensional solid with two congruent, parallel circular bases connected by a curved lateral surface; generated by rotating a rectangle around one of its sides.

Disk: A filled circular region in a plane, including all points within and on the boundary of a circle; when rotated around an axis, a disk generates a sphere or cylinder depending on axis placement.

Edge: A line segment that forms the boundary or side of a two-dimensional figure; in solids of revolution, the axis of rotation is often placed along an edge of the figure.

External Axis: An axis of rotation that lies outside the boundary of the two-dimensional figure being rotated, producing a solid with a hole or hollow region through its center.

Figure: A geometric shape or set of points in a plane or space; in this lesson, a figure refers to the two-dimensional shape being rotated around an axis to produce a three-dimensional solid.

Line Segment: A portion of a line with two defined endpoints and a fixed length; the sides and edges of two-dimensional figures are line segments.

Parallel: Describes two lines, segments, or planes that are always the same distance apart and never intersect; in a cylinder, the two circular bases lie in parallel planes.

Perpendicular: Describes two lines, segments, or planes that intersect at a right angle \( (90^\circ) \); the legs of a right triangle are perpendicular to each other.

Plane: A flat, two-dimensional surface that extends infinitely in all directions; a two-dimensional figure lies in a plane, and a cross-section of a three-dimensional solid is a planar slice.

Radius: The distance from the center of a circle to any point on its boundary; when a point in a 2D figure rotates around an axis, its distance from the axis equals the radius of the circular path it traces.

Rectangle: A quadrilateral with four right angles and opposite sides that are parallel and equal in length; when rotated around one of its sides, a rectangle generates a cylinder.

Revolution: A complete \( 360^\circ \) rotation of a figure around an axis; one full revolution sweeps the figure through all positions to generate a closed three-dimensional solid.

Rotation: A transformation that turns a figure by a specified angle around a fixed point or axis; in three-dimensional geometry, rotating a 2D figure around an axis sweeps it through space to generate a solid.

Solid of Revolution: A three-dimensional figure formed by rotating a two-dimensional figure \( 360^\circ \) around a fixed axis.

Sphere: A three-dimensional solid in which every point on the surface is equidistant from the center; generated by rotating a semicircle around its diameter.

Square: A rectangle with all four sides equal in length and all four angles equal to \( 90^\circ \); a special case of a rectangle that generates a cylinder with equal radius and height when rotated around one of its sides.

Three-Dimensional: Having length, width, and depth; a three-dimensional figure occupies space and cannot be contained in a single plane, in contrast to a two-dimensional figure which is flat.

Torus: A donut-shaped three-dimensional solid generated by rotating a circle or other closed curve around an external axis that does not intersect the figure.

Two-Dimensional Figure: A flat shape with length and width but no depth; examples include rectangles, triangles, semicircles, and trapezoids, which serve as the starting figures in generating solids of revolution.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In this lesson, you will explore how rotating a flat, two-dimensional figure around a fixed line — called an axis of rotation — generates a three-dimensional solid. This type of solid is called a solid of revolution. You have already seen three-dimensional solids like cylinders, cones, and spheres. In this lesson, you will discover that each of those shapes is simply what you get when you spin a specific flat figure in a complete circle. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to visualize what solid a given 2D figure produces when rotated, identify how the position and orientation of the axis of rotation controls the shape of the resulting solid, predict and sketch solids of revolution from described rotations, and connect these ideas to objects you encounter every day.

What is a Solid of Revolution?

Imagine holding a rectangular piece of cardboard flat on a table, then spinning it quickly around one of its edges. As it spins, every point on the cardboard traces a circular path. If it spins all the way around — a full \( 360^\circ \) — those circular paths together carve out a three-dimensional shape in space. That shape is the solid of revolution generated by the rectangle. The line the figure spins around is called the axis of rotation. The axis stays fixed while every other point in the 2D figure sweeps out a circle whose radius equals that point's distance from the axis. The farther a point is from the axis, the larger the circle it traces. Points that lie directly on the axis stay in place and trace a circle with radius zero — they become the central spine of the resulting solid.

Key Idea — How a Solid of Revolution Is Formed When a two-dimensional figure is rotated \( 360^\circ \) around a fixed axis, every point in the figure traces a circle. The collection of all those circles fills in a three-dimensional solid. The shape of that solid depends on two things: (1) the shape of the original 2D figure, and (2) the location and direction of the axis of rotation.

You can think of the 2D figure as a cross-section — a slice — of the resulting solid. If you were to cut the finished 3D solid with a flat plane that contains the axis of rotation, the cut face would match the original 2D figure (or a mirrored copy of it, depending on where the axis is placed).

Visualizing the Sweep

A helpful strategy: pick one edge or point of the 2D figure and ask, "What circle does this point trace when the figure spins?" The point at the greatest distance from the axis traces the outermost edge of the 3D solid. A point lying on the axis traces a dot — it stays still. A point on the edge farthest from the axis sweeps out the outer boundary of the solid. Together, all the circles traced by all points define the solid's full surface.


Rotating Around an Edge that lies on an Axis:

Use the simulators above to visualize each of these.

Rectangle → Cylinder

Place a rectangle so that one of its vertical sides lies exactly on the \( y \)-axis. When you rotate the rectangle \( 360^\circ \) around the \( y \)-axis, the left edge (on the axis) stays still. The right edge — which is parallel to the axis and a horizontal distance \( r \) away — sweeps out the curved lateral surface of a cylinder. The top and bottom edges sweep out the two circular bases. The resulting solid is a cylinder whose radius equals the width of the rectangle and whose height equals the height of the rectangle.

Right Triangle → Cone

Now place a right triangle so that one of its legs lies on the \( y \)-axis. When the triangle rotates \( 360^\circ \) around that leg, the vertical leg (on the axis) stays fixed. The horizontal leg — the base of the triangle — sweeps out the circular base of a cone, with radius equal to the length of the horizontal leg. The hypotenuse sweeps out the slanted lateral surface that tapers to a point (the apex) at the top, where the axis meets the triangle's opposite vertex. The resulting solid is a cone whose base radius equals the horizontal leg and whose height equals the vertical leg.

What Controls the Dimensions?

For a rectangle rotated around one side: the radius of the cylinder equals the perpendicular distance from the axis to the opposite side, and the height equals the length of the side parallel to the axis.

For a right triangle rotated around one leg: the radius of the cone equals the length of the other leg (perpendicular to the axis), and the height equals the length of the leg on the axis.

Rotating a Rectangle Example

A rectangle has a width of \( 4 \) units and a height of \( 7 \) units. It is positioned with its left side on the \( y \)-axis and rotated \( 360^\circ \) around the \( y \)-axis.

Identify the solid produced and state its dimensions. Use the simulators to help understand the dimensions.

Step 1 — Identify the figure and axis. The 2D figure is a rectangle. The axis of rotation is the \( y \)-axis, running along the left side of the rectangle (an edge of the figure).

Step 2 — Determine the solid. A rectangle rotated around one of its sides produces a cylinder.

Step 3 — Determine the dimensions.

Radius of the cylinder: the width of the rectangle = \( 4 \) units (the perpendicular distance from the axis to the opposite side).

Height of the cylinder: the height of the rectangle = \( 7 \) units.

Solid produced: A cylinder with radius \( r = 4 \) units and height \( h = 7 \) units.



Rotating a Right Triangle Example

A right triangle has legs of length \( 3 \) units (horizontal) and \( 5 \) units (vertical). The vertical leg is placed on the \( y \)-axis and the triangle is rotated \( 360^\circ \) around the \( y \)-axis.

Identify the solid produced and state its dimensions. Use the simulators to help understand the dimensions.

Step 1 — Identify the figure and axis. The 2D figure is a right triangle. The axis of rotation is the \( y \)-axis, running along the vertical leg.

Step 2 — Determine the solid. A right triangle rotated around one of its legs produces a cone.

Step 3 — Determine the dimensions.

Base radius of the cone: length of the horizontal leg = \( 3 \) units.

Height of the cone: length of the vertical leg (on the axis) = \( 5 \) units.

Solid produced: A cone with base radius \( r = 3 \) units and height \( h = 5 \) units.



Rotating a Semicircle: The Sphere

A semicircle is exactly half of a circle. Use the simulator above to visualize this.

If you place the diameter of the semicircle along the \( x \)-axis and rotate the semicircle \( 360^\circ \) around that diameter, the curved arc sweeps out the full surface of a sphere. Every point on the arc is the same distance — the radius \( r \) — from the center of the diameter, so every point traces a circle of the same radius \( r \). Together, those circles fill in a perfect sphere.

Key Idea — Sphere Dimensions

When a semicircle of radius \( r \) is rotated \( 360^\circ \) around its diameter, the resulting sphere has radius \( r \). The diameter of the semicircle becomes the axis of rotation and also equals the diameter of the sphere.

Rotating a Semicircle Example

A semicircle has a radius of \( 6 \) units. Its diameter is placed along the \( x \)-axis and it is rotated \( 360^\circ \) around the \( x \)-axis.

Identify the solid and its key dimension. Use the simulators to help understand the dimensions.

Step 1 — Identify the figure and axis. The 2D figure is a semicircle. The axis of rotation is the \( x \)-axis, running along the diameter of the semicircle.

Step 2 — Determine the solid. A semicircle rotated around its diameter produces a sphere.

Step 3 — Determine the dimension. The radius of the sphere equals the radius of the semicircle: \( r = 6 \) units.

Solid produced: A sphere with radius \( r = 6 \) units.



How the Axis Location Changes the Solid

The most powerful idea in this lesson is that the position of the axis of rotation — not just the shape of the 2D figure — determines the solid that is produced. The axis can be placed in three general positions relative to the 2D figure:

  1. Along an edge of the figure. The edge on the axis stays in place; the rest of the figure sweeps around it. This is the case for a rectangle → cylinder or a right triangle → cone.
  2. Through the interior of the figure. The axis passes through the figure itself, dividing it. Points on each side of the axis sweep out circles, producing a solid that has no hollow interior.
  3. External to the figure — the axis does not touch the figure at all. Every point of the figure is at least some minimum distance from the axis, so the innermost points sweep out circles with nonzero radius. The resulting solid has a hole through its center, like a donut. This is called a torus when the cross-section is a circle, or more generally a solid with a hollow core.

External Axis — Hollow Solids

When the axis of rotation does not touch the 2D figure, the resulting solid will have a hole through it. The size of the hole depends on how far the closest edge of the figure is from the axis. The farther the figure is from the axis, the larger the hollow core of the resulting solid.

Example: A rectangle whose nearest side is \( 2 \) units from the axis and whose farthest side is \( 5 \) units from the axis will produce a hollow cylinder (cylindrical shell) with inner radius \( 2 \) units and outer radius \( 5 \) units.


Rotating a Rectangle Example

A rectangle has a width of \( 3 \) units and a height of \( 5 \) units. Its nearest side is \( 2 \) units from the vertical axis of rotation, and the axis does not touch the rectangle. The rectangle is rotated \( 360^\circ \) around that vertical axis. Identify the solid and describe its dimensions.

Step 1 — Identify the axis position. The axis is external — it does not touch the rectangle. The nearest side is \( 2 \) units from the axis; the farthest side is \( 2 + 3 = 5 \) units from the axis.

Step 2 — Determine the solid. Since the axis is external, the rectangle sweeps out a hollow cylinder (a cylindrical shell). The inner cavity is a cylinder of radius \( 2 \) units; the outer boundary is a cylinder of radius \( 5 \) units.

Step 3 — State the dimensions.

Inner radius: \( 2 \) units (distance from axis to near side).

Outer radius: \( 5 \) units (distance from axis to far side).

Height: \( 5 \) units (the height of the rectangle, parallel to the axis).

Solid produced: A hollow cylinder with inner radius \( 2 \) units, outer radius \( 5 \) units, and height \( 5 \) units.

Example 2

A right triangle has a vertical leg of \( 8 \) units and a horizontal leg of \( 3 \) units. The vertical leg is placed on the \( y \)-axis and the triangle is rotated \( 360^\circ \) around the \( y \)-axis.

Use the simulators to help understand the dimensions.

(a) Name the solid that is produced.

(b) State the base radius and height of that solid.

(c) Describe in one sentence how the hypotenuse of the triangle relates to the surface of the resulting solid.

Show Solution

Step 1 — Identify the figure and axis placement. The figure is a right triangle; the axis runs along the vertical leg.

Step 2 — Name the solid. A right triangle rotated around one of its legs produces a cone.

Step 3 — Find the dimensions.

Base radius = horizontal leg = \( 3 \) units.

Height = vertical leg (on the axis) = \( 8 \) units.

Step 4 — Hypotenuse and surface. The hypotenuse sweeps out the slanted lateral surface of the cone — the slant that connects the circular base to the apex. The slant height of the cone equals the length of the hypotenuse of the triangle, which is \( \sqrt{3^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{73} \approx 8.54 \) units.

Answer:

(a) Cone

(b) Base radius \( r = 3 \) units; height \( h = 8 \) units.

(c) The hypotenuse of the triangle sweeps out the slanted lateral (side) surface of the cone, and its length becomes the slant height of the cone.



Example 3

Consider a rectangle that is \( 4 \) units wide and \( 6 \) units tall. Predict what solid of revolution is produced in each situation below, and describe how the solids differ.

Use the simulators to help understand the dimensions and separate situations.

Situation A: The rectangle's left side lies on the vertical axis of rotation.

Situation B: The rectangle's left side is \( 3 \) units to the right of the vertical axis of rotation (axis is external).

Show Solution

Situation A — Axis along the left edge:

The axis touches the rectangle's left side. The rectangle sweeps out a solid cylinder with radius \( 4 \) units and height \( 6 \) units. There is no hole — the interior of the cylinder is completely filled.

Situation B — Axis external, \( 3 \) units to the left:

The rectangle's nearest side (left) is \( 3 \) units from the axis. Its farthest side (right) is \( 3 + 4 = 7 \) units from the axis.

The rectangle sweeps out a hollow cylinder (cylindrical shell) with inner radius \( 3 \) units, outer radius \( 7 \) units, and height \( 6 \) units. A cylindrical hole of radius \( 3 \) units runs through its center.

Answer:

Situation A: Solid cylinder; radius \( 4 \) units, height \( 6 \) units — no hole.

Situation B: Hollow cylinder; inner radius \( 3 \) units, outer radius \( 7 \) units, height \( 6 \) units — has a cylindrical hole through the center.

Key Takeaway: The same rectangle generates two completely different solids depending only on where the axis is placed. Moving the axis away from the figure's edge introduces a hollow core. The farther the figure is from the axis, the larger the hole in the resulting solid.



Example 4

A semicircle with radius \( 10 \) units has its diameter placed along the \( x \)-axis. It is rotated \( 360^\circ \) around the \( x \)-axis.

Use the simulators to help understand the dimensions.

(a) Name the solid produced.

(b) State the key dimension of that solid.

(c) A classmate claims that rotating a full circle (not a semicircle) around its diameter would produce a larger solid than rotating the semicircle. Is this claim correct? Explain.

Show Solution

Part (a) — Name the solid. A semicircle rotated \( 360^\circ \) around its diameter produces a sphere.

Part (b) — Key dimension. The sphere has radius \( r = 10 \) units.

Part (c) — Full circle vs. semicircle. The claim is incorrect. When the semicircle is rotated \( 360^\circ \), its curved arc sweeps all the way around and the opposite half of the sphere is traced by the arc moving through the other \( 180^\circ \) of rotation. By the time the rotation completes a full \( 360^\circ \), the arc has swept through both hemispheres and the entire sphere is filled. Rotating a full circle (both halves above and below the diameter) around the same axis produces the exact same sphere — the bottom half of the circle traces paths that are already covered by rotating the top half. The solid is identical in both cases.

Answer:

(a) Sphere

(b) Radius \( r = 10 \) units.

(c) The claim is incorrect. Rotating a semicircle \( 360^\circ \) already produces the complete sphere. Adding the other half of the circle does not add any new surface or volume — the rotation already covers both hemispheres.



Solids of Revolution in the Real-World

Solids of revolution are everywhere in the designed world. A potter spinning clay on a wheel is literally generating a solid of revolution — the cross-sectional profile of the bowl or vase is the 2D shape being rotated. A lathe in a machine shop spins a piece of metal or wood around an axis while a cutting tool shapes the profile, generating any solid of revolution the craftsperson designs. Rocket nose cones, wine glasses, pulleys, wheels, and the lenses in a camera are all solids of revolution. Even a tomato — approximately spherical — can be thought of as a solid formed by rotating a semicircular outline around a vertical axis.

Understanding which 2D shape produces a given 3D solid is useful in engineering and manufacturing because it tells you exactly what cross-sectional profile to cut if you want a specific finished shape. The reverse is also valuable: if you see a 3D object and want to describe or reproduce it, identifying its cross-section gives you the 2D template you need.

Example 5

A potter is shaping a cylindrical vase on a spinning wheel. The profile she holds against the spinning clay is a rectangle that is \( 2 \) inches wide and \( 10 \) inches tall. The inner edge of the rectangle rests on the axis of the spinning wheel, and the outer edge is \( 2 \) inches away from that axis.

(a) What three-dimensional solid does the spinning clay form?

(b) State the dimensions of the resulting solid.

(c) The potter now moves her rectangular profile so that its inner edge is \( 1 \) inch from the axis instead of touching it. Describe how the resulting solid changes.

Show Solution

Part (a). The rectangle has its left side on the axis of rotation. A rectangle rotated around one of its sides produces a cylinder.

Part (b).

Radius = \( 2 \) inches (width of rectangle, measured from the axis to the outer edge).

Height = \( 10 \) inches (height of the rectangle).

Part (c). With the inner edge now \( 1 \) inch from the axis, the axis is external to the rectangle. The inner edge sweeps out a circle of radius \( 1 \) inch (a hollow core), and the outer edge sweeps out a circle of radius \( 1 + 2 = 3 \) inches. The resulting solid is a hollow cylinder with a cylindrical hole of radius \( 1 \) inch through its center. This corresponds to a vase or tube with a wall thickness of \( 2 \) inches rather than a solid cylinder of clay.

Answer:

(a) Cylinder (solid)

(b) Radius \( r = 2 \) inches, height \( h = 10 \) inches.

(c) The solid becomes a hollow cylinder with inner radius \( 1 \) inch, outer radius \( 3 \) inches, and height \( 10 \) inches — modeling a thick-walled tube rather than a solid rod of clay.

Connection: This is exactly what happens when a potter creates a vase with walls rather than a solid block of clay. Shifting the cross-section profile away from the axis creates the hollow interior. The axis of rotation corresponds to the center of the spinning wheel, and the distance from the axis to the clay profile determines the wall location.



Conclusion

In this lesson, you learned that rotating a two-dimensional figure \( 360^\circ \) around a fixed axis of rotation generates a three-dimensional solid of revolution. The shape of the solid depends on two factors: the shape of the original 2D figure, and the position of the axis of rotation relative to that figure. When the axis runs along an edge of the figure, you get familiar solids — cylinders from rectangles, cones from right triangles, and spheres from semicircles. When the axis is external — placed outside the figure entirely — the resulting solid has a hollow core, and the inner and outer radii are determined by the distances from the axis to the nearest and farthest edges of the figure. You practiced identifying solids from described rotations, determining key dimensions, and reasoning about how shifting the axis changes the resulting shape. These ideas connect directly to real-world manufacturing, design, and art: any object with rotational symmetry around a central axis — from vases and wheels to rocket nose cones and lenses — is a solid of revolution, and the cross-sectional profile that generated it is its defining 2D shape.
