📚 VOCABULARY

Center: The fixed point in a circle that is equidistant from every point on the circle.

Circle: The set of all points in a plane that are a fixed distance (radius) from a given point (center).

Coordinate Plane: A two-dimensional surface formed by the intersection of a horizontal number line (x-axis) and a vertical number line (y-axis).

Diameter: A line segment that passes through the center of a circle and has endpoints on the circle; it is twice the length of the radius.

Distance Formula: A formula used to find the distance between two points in the coordinate plane \(d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\).

Equation: A mathematical statement that shows two expressions are equal.

Graph: A visual representation of mathematical relationships on the coordinate plane.

Model: A representation of a real-world situation using mathematical concepts such as equations, diagrams, or graphs.

Origin: The point \((0,0)\) where the x-axis and y-axis intersect on the coordinate plane.

Point: An exact location in space, usually represented as a coordinate pair \((x,y)\) on the plane.

Radius: A line segment from the center of a circle to any point on the circle; half the length of the diameter.

Standard Form (of a Circle's Equation): The equation \((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2\), where \((h,k)\) is the center and \(r\) is the radius.

Translation: A type of transformation that slides a figure a certain distance in a given direction on the coordinate plane.

x-coordinate: The horizontal value in a pair of coordinates; it tells how far left or right the point is from the origin.

y-coordinate: The vertical value in a pair of coordinates; it tells how far up or down the point is from the origin.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore how to derive the standard form of a circle’s equation using the Pythagorean Theorem. You will learn how the location of a circle’s center and its radius on the coordinate plane help us express the relationship between any point on the circle and its center. This powerful connection allows us to represent circles algebraically and apply this understanding to both mathematical and real-world situations.


Deriving the Equation of a Circle Using the Pythagorean Theorem

To begin, recall that a circle is the set of all points that are a fixed distance (the radius) from a central point (the center).

On the coordinate plane, we can describe the location of the center with a point \((h,k)\). Suppose we select any point \((x,y)\) that lies on the circle. The distance from this point to the center is the radius, \(r\).

Using the Distance Formula, we can calculate the distance between \((h,k)\) and \((x,y)\), also known as the length of the radius.

\(r=\sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2}\)

Then, if we square both sides to eliminate the square root, we get the standard form of an equation of a circle.

\(r^2=(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2\)

This is known as the standard form of a circle’s equation. It is directly derived from the Pythagorean Theorem by recognizing that the distance between any point \((x,y)\) on the circle and the center \((h,k)\) forms a right triangle with legs \((x-h)\) and \((y-k)\), and a hypotenuse equal to the radius \(r\).


Writing the Equation of a Circle Given Different Key Features

Once we understand the structure of the equation, we can write it using various given features. Let's explore some various examples.


Given the center and the radius:

Plug the values of \((h,k)\)and \(r\) into the standard form \((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2\).


Example 1

A circle with the center \((5,-2)\) and radius \(4\) is shown.

Write the equation in standard form that represents the circle.

Solution:

  • Standard Form of the equation of a circle uses the center and the radius to fill in the values for h, k, and r in the equation \((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2\).
  • From the graph of the circle, we know that the center is \((5,-2)\).
  • This means that \(h=5\) and \(k=-2\).
  • We also know that the radius of the circle is 4. This means \(r=4\).
  • We can take all these values and write the equation of the circle.
  • \((x-5)^2+(y+2)^2=4^2\)

Answer: The equation that represents the circle is \((x-5)^2+(y+2)^2=16\).



Given the center and a point on the circle:

First, use the Distance Formula to find the radius by calculating the distance between the center and the point. Then use that radius in the standard form.



Example 2

A circle with the center \((-4,-3)\) and radius \(2\sqrt{13}\) is shown.

Write the equation in standard form that represents the circle.

Solution:

  • Standard Form of the equation of a circle uses the center and the radius to fill in the values for h, k, and r in the equation \((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2\).
  • From the graph of the circle, we know that the center is \((-4,-3)\).
  • This means that \(h=-4\) and \(k=-3\).
  • We also know that the radius of the circle is \(2\sqrt{13}\). This means \(r=2\sqrt{13}\).
  • We can take all these values and write the equation of the circle.
  • \((x+4)^2+(y+3)^2=(2\sqrt{13})^2\)
  • To evaluate \((2\sqrt{13})^2\), we square both pieces of the number.
    • \((2\sqrt{13})^2 \implies 2^2\cdot \sqrt{13}^2 \implies 4\cdot 13 \implies 52\)

Answer: The equation that represents the circle is \((x+4)^2+(y+3)^2=52\).


Example 3

A circle with a center of \((-3,1)\) and a point on the circle at \((-11,-5)\) is shown.

Write the equation that represents the circle.

Solution:

  • To write the equation of the circle that represents the graph shown, in standard form, we need to know the center and the radius.
  • We know the center is \((-3,1)\). Thus, \(h=-3\) and \(k=1\).
  • However, we do not know the value of the radius.
  • We do have the coordinates of another point on the circle. Therefore, we can use the distance formula from the center to that point to find the length of the radius.
  • We have \((-3,1)\rightarrow (x_1,y_1)\) and \((-11,-5)\rightarrow (x_2,y_2)\).
  • Length of Radius \(=\sqrt{(-11-(-3))^2+(-5-1)^2} =\sqrt{(-8)^2+(-6)^2} =\sqrt{64+36} =\sqrt{100} =10\)
  • Using the distance formula, we find that \(r=10\).
  • Now, we can write the equation.
    • \((x+3)^2+(y-1)^2=10^2\)

Answer: The equation that represents the circle shown is \((x+3)^2+(y-1)^2=100\).



Given two points on the circle that form a diameter:

First, find the midpoint of the two points to determine the center. Then, use the Distance Formula to calculate the radius (half the distance between the two points).



Example 4

A circle whose diameter has end points \((9,12)\) and \((13,-8)\) is shown.

Write the equation that represents the circle.

Solution:

  • To write an equation of a circle, we need two pieces of information. We need the coordinates of the center and the length of the radius.
  • To find the coordinates of the center, we know that the center is the middle of the circle.
  • Since we have the coordinates of the endpoints for the diameter, we can find the midpoint of the diameter, which will be the center of the circle.
  • Center \(=(\frac{9+13}{2},\frac{12+-8}{2})=(\frac{22}{2},\frac{4}{2})=(11,2)\)
  • We now know that the center point is \((11,2)\). Therefore, \(h=11\) and \(k=2\).
  • Now, we can find the length of the radius using one of the endpoints and the center.
  • \((11,2)=(x_1,y_1)\) and \((9,12)=(x_2,y_2)\)
  • Length of Radius \(=\sqrt{(9-11)^2+(12-2)^2} =\sqrt{(-2)^2+(10)^2} =\sqrt{4+100} =\sqrt{104}\).
  • Now, we can use the center \((h,k)=(11,2)\) and the radius \(r=\sqrt{104}\) to write the equation of the circle.
    • \((x-11)^2+(y-2)^2=(\sqrt{104})^2\)

Answer: The equation that represents the circle is \((x-11)^2+(y-2)^2=104\).



Modeling Real-World Circles Using Equations

In real-world contexts, circles can model a variety of circular objects or paths, such as fountains, round tables, wheels, and radar coverage.


Example 5

Suppose a circular garden has a center located at \((5,3)\) on a blueprint and extends out 7 feet in every direction.

Create an equation that represents the circular garden.

Solution:

We know the center \((h,k)=(5,3)\) and we know the length of the radius \(r=7\).

The equation that represents the garden is \((x-5)^2+(y-3)^2=7^2\) or \((x-5)^2+(y-3)^2=49\).

This model allows us to communicate the location and size of the garden precisely using geometry and algebra. In the following lesson, we learn how to create a graphical model of this garden and solve problems involving the garden.



Conclusion

The equation of a circle is more than a formula—it is a bridge between algebra and geometry. By connecting the Pythagorean Theorem to the definition of a circle, we are able to derive the standard form and use it to represent circles in both mathematical and real-world contexts. Whether you’re given a center and a radius, a point on the circle, or the endpoints of a diameter, you can write the equation of the circle confidently. This foundational skill will prepare you for upcoming lessons, where you'll graph circles and use equations to solve problems involving circular shapes.
